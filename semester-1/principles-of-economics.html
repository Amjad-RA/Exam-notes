10-minute cheat sheet

Every definition, list and diagram name that earns marks — scan this outside the hall.

MODULE ITen principles (Mankiw)

  • Economics (Mankiw): “the study of how society manages its scarce resources.”
  • How people make decisions: (1) people face trade-offs · (2) the cost of something is what you give up — opportunity cost · (3) rational people think at the margin · (4) people respond to incentives.
  • How people interact: (5) trade can make everyone better off · (6) markets are usually a good way to organise activity — the invisible hand · (7) governments can sometimes improve outcomes — market failure (externality, market power).
  • How the economy works: (8) living standards depend on productivity · (9) prices rise when government prints too much money · (10) short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment — Phillips curve (A. W. Phillips, 1958).
  • Efficiency vs equality: the classic trade-off — bigger pie vs fairer slices.

MODULE IIThinking like an economist

  • Scientific method: observation → theory (hypothesis) → more observation (test with data) → accept / revise.
  • Assumptions simplify the world (e.g., ceteris paribus, two goods). Model = simplified representation of reality, often a diagram or equation. Function = Y = f(X), dependent vs independent variables.
  • Circular flow: households ↔ firms through goods & services market and factor market; real flow one way, money flow the other. 3-sector adds government (taxes, purchases, transfers).
  • PPF: combinations of two goods producible with given resources & technology. Shows scarcity (beyond = unattainable), choice (points on it), opportunity cost (slope = MRT), efficiency (on it) vs inefficiency (inside), growth (outward shift). Bowed out → increasing opportunity cost.
  • Micro = households, firms, markets · Macro = economy-wide (inflation, unemployment, growth).
  • Positive (what is — testable) vs normative (what ought to be — value judgement).
  • Why economists disagree: different scientific judgements · different values · perception vs reality (they agree more than it seems).

MODULE IIILimits, alternatives & choices

  • Economics (McConnell): “the social science concerned with how individuals, institutions and society make optimal choices under conditions of scarcity.”
  • Economic perspective: scarcity & choice · purposeful behaviour (people seek utility/profit; decisions are deliberate, not random) · marginal analysis (compare MB with MC; act while MB ≥ MC).
  • Individual's economizing problem: limited income, unlimited wants → budget line; attainable vs unattainable; trade-offs; opportunity cost = slope.
  • Society's economizing problem: scarce resources — land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship (rent, wages, interest, profit) — vs unlimited wants → PPF; full employment & full production assumed.
  • Growth & the future: more resources or better technology shift the PPF out; present choices (more capital goods now) → future possibilities; international trade lets a nation consume beyond its PPF.
  • Why study economics: economics for citizenship, personal decisions, business, policy.

MODULE IVThe market system

  • Economic systems: laissez-faire capitalism (no government role beyond property & law; “let it be”) · command system (public ownership, central planning — former USSR, North Korea) · market system (private ownership, markets & prices; limited government).
  • 9 characteristics of the market system: private property · freedom of enterprise & choice · self-interest · competition · markets & prices · technology & capital goods · specialization · use of money · active but limited government.
  • 5 fundamental questions: What will be produced? How? Who gets the output? How will the system accommodate change? How will it promote progress?
  • Invisible hand (Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations, 1776): self-interest guided by competition promotes society's interest.
  • Demise of command systems: the coordination problem & the incentive problem.
  • Risk: the profit system rewards risk-bearing owners; firms shield employees and suppliers (fixed wages, prices); restricting risk to owners lets workers and suppliers focus, encourages enterprise and investment.

Module I · 10 hours · 21 marks · Mankiw ch. 1

Basic Principles of Economics

How people make decisionsHow people interactHow the economy as a whole works
★ Golden definition
“Economics is the study of how society manages its scarce resources.”
— N. Gregory Mankiw. Scarcity: society has limited resources and therefore cannot produce all the goods and services people wish to have.

1.1The ten principles at a glance

1.2Trade-offs and opportunity cost (principles 1–2)

“There is no such thing as a free lunch.” Getting more of one thing means giving up something else. The opportunity cost of an item is whatever must be given up to obtain it — the value of the next-best alternative.

Individuals

A student's hour: study economics, study history, or sleep. The cost of college is not just fees but the wages forgone.

Families

Income spent on food and clothes vs a holiday vs saving for children's education.

Society

“Guns vs butter” (defence vs consumer goods); clean environment vs higher incomes; efficiency vs equality — the size of the pie vs how fairly it is shared.

1.3Thinking at the margin and responding to incentives (principles 3–4)

Rational people systematically do the best they can. They make decisions by comparing marginal benefit (MB) and marginal cost (MC) — the small, incremental adjustments to a plan. Act only if MB > MC.

Classic examples
  • Airline seat: a flight costs ₹10,000 per seat on average, but if a seat is empty, selling it to a stand-by passenger for ₹3,000 is worthwhile because the marginal cost is tiny.
  • Water and diamonds: water is essential but cheap because its marginal unit is abundant; diamonds are inessential but dear because the marginal unit is rare.
  • Incentives: a petrol tax encourages smaller cars and public transport; seat-belt laws can make drivers less careful (Peltzman effect).

1.4How people interact (principles 5–7)

5 · Trade

Trade is not a contest with winners and losers. It lets each person or country specialise in what it does best and enjoy a greater variety of goods at lower cost.

6 · Markets & the invisible hand

In a market economy, decentralised decisions of firms and households guided by prices allocate resources. Adam Smith's invisible hand: self-interest, guided by prices, promotes general well-being. Central planning failed because planners lack the information prices carry.

7 · Government

Government enforces property rights and can improve outcomes when there is market failure — a market left alone fails to allocate resources efficiently. Causes: externalities (pollution), market power (monopoly). Also promotes equality (taxes, welfare).

1.5How the economy as a whole works (principles 8–10)

8 · Productivity

Differences in living standards across countries and over time are explained by productivity — goods and services produced per unit of labour input. Policy should raise education, capital and technology.

9 · Money and inflation

Inflation is an increase in the overall level of prices. When government prints too much money, the value of money falls: more money chases the same goods, so prices rise (e.g., Germany 1921–23, Zimbabwe in the 2000s).

Module II · 10 hours · 21 marks · Mankiw ch. 2

Thinking like an Economist

Scientific methodAssumptions & modelsCircular flowPPFMicro / macroPositive / normativeWhy economists disagree
★ Golden definition
“An economic model is a simplified representation of reality, built on assumptions, that economists use to explain how the economy works and to predict the effects of change.”
Economists act as scientists when they explain the world (positive) and as policy advisers when they try to improve it (normative).

2.1The economist as scientist

Assumptions

Simplify a complex world to make it easier to understand. A physicist assumes a marble falls in a vacuum; an economist assumes two countries and two goods to study trade, or ceteris paribus — other things equal. Good assumptions fit the question.

Models

Diagrams and equations that leave out detail to show what really matters — like a plastic model of the human body in a biology class. Examples: circular flow, PPF.

Functions

A function shows how one variable depends on others: Y = f(X). Demand function Qd = f(P, income, tastes…); consumption function C = f(Y). X is independent, Y dependent.

2.2The circular-flow diagram

A visual model of how money and real things flow through markets among households, firms and, in the three-sector version, government.

Real flow
  • Households supply land, labour, capital to firms
  • Firms supply goods and services to households
Money flow (opposite direction)
  • Firms pay wages, rent, interest, profit
  • Households spend income on goods and services

2.3The production possibilities frontier (PPF)

A graph showing the combinations of output the economy can possibly produce, given the available factors of production and technology. Also called the production possibility curve.

What the PPF shows
  • Scarcity — points like W lie beyond reach
  • Choice — society must pick one point (A, B, C or D)
  • Opportunity cost — moving from B to C gains consumer goods but gives up capital goods
  • Efficiency — on the curve all resources are used; U inside means unemployment or waste
  • Increasing opportunity cost — the bowed-out shape: resources are not equally suited to both goods
  • Marginal rate of transformation (MRT) — the slope: how much of one good must be given up for one more unit of the other
  • Growth — more resources or better technology shift the PPF outward (dashed)

2.4Micro vs macro · positive vs normative

BasisMicroeconomicsMacroeconomics
StudiesHow households and firms make decisions and interact in specific marketsEconomy-wide phenomena
ExamplesPrice of rice, a firm's output, rent control in a cityInflation, unemployment, GDP growth, national debt
ApproachIndividual unitsAggregates
Positive statement
  • Describes the world as it is
  • Can be tested with evidence
  • “Minimum-wage laws cause unemployment.”
Normative statement
  • Prescribes how the world ought to be
  • Involves value judgements
  • “The government should raise the minimum wage.”

Why economists' advice is not always followed: policymakers weigh political feasibility, other advisers, interest groups and public opinion; the economist is only one voice.

2.5Why economists disagree

Differences in scientific judgements

They disagree on which theory is right or on the size of key effects — e.g., whether taxing consumption or income gives more saving.

Differences in values

Same facts, different values — how much equality to trade for efficiency, or who should bear a tax.

Perception vs reality

They agree far more than it seems. Most accept that rent control reduces the quantity and quality of housing, and that tariffs reduce general welfare — yet such policies persist for political reasons.

Module III · 10 hours · 21 marks · McConnell ch. 1

Limits, Alternatives and Choices

Economic perspectiveIndividual's economizing problemSociety's economizing problemGrowth & the future
★ Golden definition
“Economics is the social science concerned with how individuals, institutions and society make optimal (best) choices under conditions of scarcity.”
— McConnell, Brue & Flynn. The economizing problem: unlimited wants vs limited resources.

3.1The economic perspective

Scarcity and choice

Resources are scarce, so choices must be made, and every choice has an opportunity cost. “There is no free lunch” — even a “free” sample uses resources.

Purposeful behaviour

People make decisions with a goal: consumers seek utility (satisfaction), firms seek profit, governments seek public benefit. It is rational self-interest, not selfishness; it does not mean people never err.

Marginal analysis

Most choices are about “a little more or a little less”. Compare marginal benefit with marginal cost; a choice is worth making when MB ≥ MC.

3.2The individual's economizing problem

Limited income, unlimited wants

A person's budget line shows all combinations of two goods that can be bought with a fixed income at given prices. With ₹1,200, films at ₹200 and books at ₹100: 6 films, or 12 books, or any mix on the line.

  • Attainable — on or inside the line; unattainable — beyond it
  • Trade-off — more films means fewer books
  • Opportunity cost — 1 film = 2 books (the slope)
  • Choice — the best point depends on preferences
  • Higher income shifts the line outward
Society's economizing problem

Society's resources — the factors of production — are scarce:

FactorPayment
Land (natural resources)Rent
LabourWages
Capital (man-made aids)Interest
Entrepreneurial abilityProfit

PPF assumptions: full employment, fixed resources, fixed technology, two goods. Optimal allocation: produce where MB = MC.

3.3Unemployment, growth and the future

A growing economy
  • Unemployment puts the economy inside the PPF
  • Increase in resource supplies — population, capital, discoveries — shifts the PPF out
  • Advances in technology — better methods produce more with the same resources
Present choices, future possibilities

An economy that chooses more capital goods today (factories, roads, education) sacrifices some consumption now but enjoys a larger PPF later. Compare “Presentville” (consumes more now) with “Futureville” (invests more now, grows faster).

A qualification: international tradeThrough specialisation and trade, a nation can consume a combination of goods beyond its own PPF — importing what others produce more cheaply. Trade also brings technology and larger markets, boosting growth (e.g., India's software exports since the 1990s).
Module IV · 15 hours · 35 marks · McConnell ch. 2

The Market System

Economic systems9 characteristics5 fundamental questionsInvisible handRisk
★ Golden definition
“The market system (capitalism) is characterised by the private ownership of resources and the use of markets and prices to coordinate and direct economic activity.”
An economic system is a particular set of institutional arrangements and a coordinating mechanism for producing goods and services.

4.1Economic systems

BasisCommand systemMarket system
OwnershipGovernment owns most property resourcesPrivate ownership
DecisionsCentral planning boardIndividuals and firms through markets
CoordinatorPlans and quotasPrices (invisible hand)
MotivePlan targetsSelf-interest, profit
CompetitionAbsentCentral
WeaknessCoordination & incentive problemsInequality, market failure

4.2Characteristics of the market system

1 · Private property

Individuals and firms own resources; the right to bequeath gives incentive to work, save and invest.

2 · Freedom of enterprise & choice

Firms may obtain resources and sell output; owners and consumers choose freely.

3 · Self-interest

Each unit pursues its own best interest — the motivating force.

4 · Competition

Many independent buyers and sellers, freedom to enter and exit — regulates self-interest.

5 · Markets & prices

Prices signal and coordinate millions of decisions.

6 · Technology & capital goods

Profit motive drives innovation and investment.

7 · Specialisation

Division of labour and geographic specialisation raise output.

8 · Use of money

Money as a medium of exchange removes the need for barter's coincidence of wants.

9 · Active but limited government

Corrects market failures and provides public goods.

4.3The five fundamental questions

QuestionHow the market system answers it
1 · What will be produced?Consumer sovereignty — consumers' “rupee votes” decide; goods that earn a profit get produced
2 · How will it be produced?With the least-cost combination of resources and technology, forced by competition
3 · Who will get the output?Those willing and able to pay — distribution follows money income
4 · How will the system accommodate change?Changes in tastes, technology and resources alter prices and profits, guiding resources to new uses
5 · How will it promote progress?Profit rewards technological advance (creative destruction) and capital accumulation

4.4The invisible hand and the demise of command systems

The invisible hand

Adam Smith (The Wealth of Nations, 1776): firms and resource suppliers seeking their own interest are, as if led by an invisible hand, promoting the public interest. Virtues: efficiency, incentives and freedom.

Why command systems failed
  • Coordination problem — planners cannot gather and process the information needed for millions of goods; one shortfall cascades into bottlenecks
  • Incentive problem — no profit, no reward for innovation or quality; output targets ignore what consumers want

4.5How the market system deals with risk

The profit system

Owners who take risks earn profits when they succeed and bear losses when they fail; the prospect of profit attracts risk-takers.

Shielding employees and suppliers

Workers get fixed wages and suppliers fixed contract prices; business risk falls on owners.

Benefits of restricting risk to owners

Attracts those best able to bear risk; lets workers and suppliers specialise without worry; encourages investment and innovation.

Type of business riskExample
Demand riskConsumer tastes change — feature phones to smartphones
Competition riskA rival launches a cheaper product
Technological riskNew technology makes a product obsolete
Input-cost riskFuel or raw-material prices rise
Economic riskRecession reduces sales
Natural & political riskFloods, pandemics, policy changes

Section A — Short answers

3 marks each~5–6 lines · 3–4 min

Formula: definition (1 mark) + two points or a mini-diagram (1 mark) + example (1 mark).

Explain trade-offs. Why do people face trade-offs? Give real-life situations.›3 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q1 · Amal 2025 Q1

A trade-off means that to get more of one thing we must give up something else. People face trade-offs because resources — time, money, materials — are scarce while wants are unlimited (“there is no such thing as a free lunch”).

  • A student chooses between studying economics, studying history or sleeping.
  • A family chooses between a holiday and saving for education.
  • Society: “guns vs butter”; clean environment vs higher income; efficiency vs equality.
Define market failure.›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q2

Market failure is a situation in which a market left on its own fails to allocate resources efficiently (Mankiw). Two main causes:

  • Externality — the impact of one person's action on a bystander (e.g., factory pollution).
  • Market power — the ability of a single seller or small group to influence prices (e.g., a monopoly).

Government can sometimes improve outcomes — principle 7.

Define laissez-faire. Point out its features. / How is laissez-faire understood in economic theory?›3 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q9 · Amal 2025 Q3

Laissez-faire capitalism (“let it be”) is an economic system in which government's role is limited to protecting private property and establishing a legal environment for the free operation of markets.

  • Private ownership of all resources; free enterprise and choice.
  • Prices and competition coordinate the economy; self-interest drives decisions.
  • No government regulation of prices, wages or production — the “invisible hand” rules.

It has never existed in pure form; real economies are mixed.

How do you explain the Phillips curve?›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q4

The Phillips curve (A. W. Phillips, 1958) shows the short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment: lower unemployment comes with higher inflation, and vice versa. Draw a downward-sloping curve with inflation on the y-axis and unemployment on the x-axis. When demand rises, firms hire more and raise prices; unemployment falls, inflation rises. The trade-off is temporary — in the long run it disappears (Mankiw's principle 10).

What is an economic model?›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q5

An economic model is a simplified representation of reality, built on assumptions, used to explain how the economy works and to predict outcomes. Models omit details to focus on what matters — like a plastic model of the human body. They take the form of diagrams or equations.

Examples: the circular-flow diagram and the production possibilities frontier.

How do you explain functions in economic theory?›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q6

A function expresses how one economic variable depends on one or more others: Y = f(X), where Y is the dependent variable and X the independent variable.

  • Demand function: Qd = f(P, income, tastes, prices of related goods).
  • Consumption function: C = f(Y) — consumption depends on income.

Functions let economists build models, draw graphs and predict how a change in one variable affects another, ceteris paribus.

What is purposeful behaviour in economic decisions?›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q7

Purposeful behaviour means people make decisions deliberately, with a goal in mind, rather than randomly. Consumers seek to maximise utility (satisfaction), firms seek profit, and governments seek public benefit. It is rational self-interest, not selfishness — giving to charity can be purposeful. People may still make mistakes because of imperfect information.

How is economic growth defined?›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q8

Economic growth is an increase in an economy's capacity to produce goods and services — a sustained rise in real output (real GDP). On a PPF diagram it is shown as an outward shift of the frontier.

Sources: increases in resource supplies (labour, capital, land) and advances in technology. Example: India's real GDP growth of around 6–8% a year in recent years.

Write a short note on the ‘invisible hand’. / Do you agree that markets are usually a good way to organise economic activity?›3 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q9 · Univ 2024 Q7

Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations (1776), argued that households and firms pursuing their own interest are, as if led by an “invisible hand”, guided by prices to promote society's well-being.

  • Prices carry information about scarcity and value, coordinating millions of decisions.
  • Competition prevents self-interest from turning into exploitation.

Yes — markets are usually efficient (principle 6); but government is needed when there is market failure.

What are command systems in an economy?›3 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q10

A command system (socialism or communism) is one in which the government owns most property resources and economic decisions are made by a central planning board.

  • The state decides what, how and for whom to produce.
  • Prices and profits play little role; output targets replace markets.

Examples: the former Soviet Union, North Korea, Cuba. Most collapsed because of the coordination and incentive problems.

How does international trade influence economic growth?›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q2

International trade lets a nation specialise in goods it produces at lower opportunity cost and import others, so it can consume a combination beyond its own PPF. Trade also widens markets (economies of scale), brings in new technology and capital, and increases competition and efficiency — all of which raise productivity and growth. Example: India's IT-services exports since the 1990s.

What are business risks? Identify the benefits of restricting business risks.›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q3

Business risk is the possibility of losses from unexpected changes — in demand, competition, technology, input costs or the wider economy. In the market system, risk is borne mainly by owners through the profit system.

Benefits of restricting risk to owners: it attracts those most willing and able to bear risk; workers and suppliers, shielded by fixed wages and contract prices, can specialise without worry; and the prospect of profit encourages investment and innovation.

‘Value judgement has a significant role in economic analysis.’ Explain.›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q4

A value judgement is a view about what is good or bad, right or wrong. It is the basis of normative economics (what ought to be), unlike positive economics (what is), which can be tested with data.

  • “Minimum-wage laws cause unemployment” — positive.
  • “The government should raise the minimum wage” — normative.

Policy advice always involves value judgements, which is one reason economists disagree.

Write a note on marginal analysis.›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q5

Marginal analysis compares the marginal benefit (extra benefit) and marginal cost (extra cost) of a small change in an activity. A rational decision-maker increases an activity as long as MB > MC and stops where MB = MC.

Example: an airline sells an empty seat to a stand-by passenger for less than the average cost because the marginal cost is very small.

Differentiate between microeconomics and macroeconomics. Give examples.›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q6

MicroeconomicsMacroeconomics
StudiesHouseholds, firms and individual marketsThe economy as a whole
ExamplesPrice of rice, a firm's output, rent controlInflation, unemployment, GDP growth

The two are linked: economy-wide outcomes arise from millions of individual decisions.

‘Rational people think at the margin.’ Explain.›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q8

Rational people systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their goals. They make decisions by thinking at the margin — comparing the marginal benefit and marginal cost of a small adjustment to a plan, and acting only if MB exceeds MC.

Example: deciding whether to study one more hour before an exam; the water–diamond paradox is explained by marginal value.

Illustrate the marginal rate of transformation using a production possibilities frontier.›3 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q10

The marginal rate of transformation (MRT) is the amount of one good that must be given up to produce one more unit of the other — the slope of the PPF.

Draw a bowed-out PPF with capital goods on the y-axis and consumer goods on the x-axis. Moving down the curve, each extra unit of consumer goods costs more units of capital goods — the MRT rises, showing increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both goods.

Explain the circular-flow model with a diagram. Is there a real flow and a money flow in an economy?›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q14 · Amal 2025 Q13

The circular-flow diagram is a visual model of the economy showing how money and real resources flow through markets among households, firms and government.

Two-sector model
  • Households own factors of production and buy goods; firms hire factors and produce goods.
  • Market for goods and services: firms sell, households buy.
  • Market for factors of production: households sell land, labour and capital; firms buy them.
Real flow vs money flow
  • Real flow: factors move from households to firms; goods and services move from firms to households.
  • Money flow (opposite direction): firms pay wages, rent, interest and profit; households spend this income on goods, which becomes firms' revenue.
Third sector: government
  • Collects taxes from households and firms.
  • Pays transfers (pensions, subsidies) and makes purchases of goods and services.

Yes — every real flow is matched by a money flow in the opposite direction; together they show how the economy's parts are interdependent.

💡 Examiner's noteThe diagram carries half the marks. Label both markets, all arrows and both flows; use a solid line for real and a dashed line for money, as in Module II above.
Explain the production possibilities frontier (PPF) and how it shows scarcity, choice and opportunity cost.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q14 · Univ 2024 Q10 (3m)

The PPF is a graph that shows the combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its available resources and technology, assuming full employment.

  1. Diagram: capital goods on the y-axis, consumer goods on the x-axis; a bowed-out curve.
  2. Scarcity: points outside the curve are unattainable with current resources.
  3. Choice: society must choose one point on the curve — more of one good means less of the other.
  4. Opportunity cost: moving along the curve, the units of one good given up to get more of the other; the slope is the MRT.
  5. Increasing opportunity cost: the bowed-out shape, because resources are specialised.
  6. Efficiency: points on the curve are efficient; points inside show unemployment or waste.
  7. Growth: more resources or better technology shift the PPF outward.

The PPF condenses the central economic problem — scarcity forces choice, and every choice has an opportunity cost — into one picture.

Illustrate the logic of marginal thinking in economics.›6 marks★ Asked 3×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q12 · Univ 2024 Q5 (3m) · Univ 2024 Q8 (3m)

Rational people think at the margin: they compare the extra benefit and extra cost of a small change rather than all-or-nothing choices.

  1. Marginal benefit (MB) — the additional benefit of one more unit; it usually falls as we consume more.
  2. Marginal cost (MC) — the additional cost of one more unit; it usually rises.
  3. Decision rule: do more while MB > MC; stop where MB = MC (draw the crossing curves).
  4. Airline example: selling an empty seat below average cost is rational because the marginal cost is tiny.
  5. Water–diamond paradox: price depends on marginal, not total, value.
  6. Everyday use: one more hour of study, one more worker, one more unit of output.

Marginal thinking turns big, confusing choices into small comparisons and is the core of the economic way of thinking.

Why do economists disagree? (Differentiate perception versus reality.)›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q15 · Univ 2024 Q17

Economists often give conflicting advice to policymakers. Mankiw gives three reasons.

  1. Differences in scientific judgements: they disagree about which theory is correct or how large an effect is — e.g., whether a consumption tax or an income tax better encourages saving.
  2. Differences in values: with the same facts, they hold different views on fairness, how much equality to trade for efficiency, or who should bear a tax.
  3. Perception versus reality: economists actually agree far more than it seems. Most agree that rent control reduces the quantity and quality of housing and that tariffs reduce general welfare. These policies persist because of politics, not because economists disagree.

Why advice is not always followed: policymakers weigh politics, interest groups and other advisers.

Disagreement mostly reflects normative values; on positive questions there is broad consensus.

What are the key steps in the scientific method of economic analysis? (Explain the role of assumptions with examples.)›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q16 · Univ 2024 Q16

Economists approach their subject with a scientist's objectivity, using the scientific method.

  1. Observation: notice a pattern, e.g., prices rising rapidly.
  2. Theory (hypothesis): propose an explanation — high money growth causes inflation.
  3. More observation: collect data to test the theory; economists rely on natural experiments from history.
  4. Evaluate: accept, revise or reject the theory.
Role of assumptions
  • Simplify a complex world so we can focus on what matters.
  • Examples: assume two countries and two goods to study trade; assume prices are fixed in the short run; ceteris paribus — all else equal.
  • The art is choosing assumptions that fit the question — like a physicist ignoring friction for a marble but not for a beach ball.

Assumptions and models let economists isolate cause and effect in a world where controlled experiments are rarely possible.

Examine the economizing problem faced by society.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q17 · Univ 2024 Q20 (10m)

Society's economizing problem is the conflict between unlimited human wants and the scarce resources available to satisfy them.

  1. Scarce resources: land (rent), labour (wages), capital (interest) and entrepreneurial ability (profit).
  2. Unlimited wants: as incomes rise, new wants appear.
  3. Choice: society must decide what to produce — shown by the PPF.
  4. Opportunity cost: producing more capital goods means fewer consumer goods; the law of increasing opportunity cost.
  5. Optimal allocation: produce each good up to where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.
  6. Full employment and efficiency: unemployment wastes resources (a point inside the PPF).

Every economy, rich or poor, must economise — make the best use of limited resources.

What are the five fundamental questions that a market system must address? (With reference to the Indian economy.)›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q18 · Univ 2024 Q13

Every economy must answer five fundamental questions; in a market system, prices and profits answer them.

  1. What will be produced? Consumer sovereignty: goods that consumers buy at profitable prices — e.g., the boom in smartphones and digital services in India.
  2. How will goods be produced? The least-cost combination of resources and technology — India's choice between labour-intensive and capital-intensive methods.
  3. Who will get the output? Those willing and able to pay — raising questions of inequality in India.
  4. How will the system accommodate change? Changing tastes and technology alter prices and profits, moving resources — e.g., from cash to UPI payments.
  5. How will the system promote progress? Profit rewards innovation and capital accumulation — start-ups, infrastructure investment.

India, as a mixed economy, answers these mainly through markets, with government correcting for inequality and market failure.

The invisible hand is a metaphor for the unseen forces that move the free-market economy. Discuss.›6 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q12

Adam Smith's “invisible hand” (The Wealth of Nations, 1776) describes how individuals pursuing their own interest are guided by prices and competition to promote the interest of society.

  1. Self-interest: firms seek profit and households seek satisfaction.
  2. Prices as signals: rising prices tell producers to supply more and consumers to economise.
  3. Competition: stops self-interest from becoming exploitation and forces efficiency.
  4. Efficiency: resources flow to their most valued uses.
  5. Incentives: rewards for effort, innovation and risk-taking.
  6. Freedom: people choose their work and consumption.

Limits: the hand fails with externalities, monopolies and inequality — hence a role for government.

The invisible hand explains why markets usually work well without central direction.

Examine the present choices and future considerations of India as a growing economy.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q11 · Univ 2024 Q2 (3m)

A nation's choice today between capital goods and consumer goods determines the position of its PPF tomorrow.

  1. Present choice: more investment in capital goods — infrastructure, factories, education — means less consumption now.
  2. Future possibility: the PPF shifts further outward, giving higher output later (“Futureville” vs “Presentville”).
  3. India's choices: heavy public investment in roads, railways and digital infrastructure; Make in India; skill development.
  4. Human capital: spending on health and education raises productivity.
  5. International trade: exports of services and imports of technology let India consume beyond its PPF and grow faster.
  6. Trade-offs: balancing growth with current welfare, employment and the environment.

By saving and investing today, India can achieve higher living standards tomorrow.

In the short run, there is a trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Explain.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q15 · Amal 2025 Q4 (3m)

Mankiw's tenth principle: society faces a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment, shown by the Phillips curve.

  1. Phillips curve: A. W. Phillips (1958) found an inverse relation between unemployment and wage inflation.
  2. Mechanism: an increase in money and spending raises demand; firms hire more workers (unemployment falls) and raise prices (inflation rises).
  3. Policy choice: governments can trade higher inflation for lower unemployment, or vice versa, through monetary and fiscal policy.
  4. Diagram: inflation on the y-axis, unemployment on the x-axis; downward-sloping curve.
  5. Short run only: prices are sticky in the short run; in the long run, expectations adjust and the trade-off disappears.

The trade-off is a key consideration in managing the business cycle.

If the government prints too much currency, it can lead to inflation. Give the reason, narrating the process of price rise.›6 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q18

Mankiw's ninth principle: prices rise when the government prints too much money.

  1. More money: the government finances spending by printing currency.
  2. More spending: people have more money to spend.
  3. Same goods: output cannot rise as fast, so “too much money chases too few goods”.
  4. Prices rise: sellers raise prices; the value of each unit of money falls.
  5. Spiral: workers demand higher wages, costs rise, prices rise again.
  6. Examples: Germany 1921–23 and Zimbabwe in the 2000s suffered hyperinflation.

In the long run, high inflation is almost always caused by excessive growth in the money supply.

Examine the significance of studying economics. Why is it important?›6 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q11

Economics studies how individuals and society make choices under scarcity; it is useful to everyone, not just economists.

  1. Economics for citizenship: helps voters judge policies on inflation, taxes, trade and welfare.
  2. Personal applications: better decisions on spending, saving, careers and investment.
  3. Business: pricing, production, hiring and investment decisions.
  4. Policy: governments use economics to design budgets, monetary policy and development plans.
  5. Understanding the world: poverty, growth, globalisation, environmental problems.
  6. Way of thinking: trade-offs, opportunity cost, marginal analysis — useful in every field.

Economics is a tool for understanding and improving both individual and social well-being.

‘The problem of scarcity and choice lies at the very heart of economics.’ Evaluate the statement, highlighting real-life situations, and discuss society's economizing problem.›10 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q20 · Amal 2025 Q17 (6m)

Economics is “the social science concerned with how individuals, institutions and society make optimal choices under conditions of scarcity” (McConnell). Because resources are limited and wants are unlimited, every individual and society must choose — scarcity and choice are indeed the heart of economics.

A. Scarcity and choice
  1. Scarcity: time, money and resources are limited relative to wants.
  2. Choice: scarcity forces people to choose among alternatives.
  3. Opportunity cost: every choice means giving up the next-best alternative — “there is no free lunch”.
  4. Real-life situations: a student's time between study and part-time work; a family's budget; a farmer's land between rice and coconut; the government's budget between defence, health and education.
B. Society's economizing problem
  1. Scarce resources: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship, rewarded by rent, wages, interest and profit.
  2. Unlimited wants: new wants appear as incomes grow.
  3. PPF: shows the combinations society can produce; points outside are unattainable (draw it).
  4. Increasing opportunity cost: the bowed-out PPF.
  5. Optimal allocation: produce up to MB = MC.
  6. Unemployment and growth: idle resources put the economy inside the PPF; more resources and better technology shift it outward.
C. Evaluation

The statement is valid: even rich countries face scarcity of clean air, time and skilled labour. Scarcity is relative, not absolute — growth reduces it but never removes it, so choice remains central.

Scarcity creates the need for choice; choice involves opportunity cost; and the way societies make these choices is the subject of economics.

Differentiate between the command system and the market system. Examine the role of private property and competition in determining market decisions and priorities.›10 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q19 · Amal 2025 Q10 (3m)

An economic system is a set of institutional arrangements and a coordinating mechanism for production. Economies differ in who owns resources and how decisions are coordinated — by central planning (command system) or by markets and prices (market system).

A. Command vs market system
BasisCommand systemMarket system
OwnershipGovernment owns most resourcesPrivate ownership
Decision-makingCentral planning boardIndividuals and firms
CoordinationPlans, quotasPrices, invisible hand
MotivePlan targetsSelf-interest, profit
CompetitionAbsentEssential

ProblemsCoordination, incentive problemsInequality, market failure
ExamplesFormer USSR, North Korea, CubaUSA, UK (mostly)
B. Role of private property
  1. Individuals and firms own and control resources, and can buy, use and sell them.
  2. The right to bequeath property encourages work, saving and investment.
  3. Owners decide how to use resources to maximise returns, so property rights drive what is produced and how.
  4. It gives the incentive to maintain and improve assets.
C. Role of competition
  1. Many independent buyers and sellers; freedom to enter and leave industries.
  2. Competition limits the power of any single firm and keeps prices close to cost.
  3. It forces firms to use least-cost methods and to innovate — this decides how goods are produced.
  4. It channels self-interest into the public interest — the invisible hand.

The collapse of command economies showed that private property and competition give the market system its efficiency and dynamism; government's role is to correct market failures, not to replace markets.

List and analyse the basic principles of economics. How do these principles guide decision-making at both the individual and societal levels?›10 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q19

Economics is the study of how society manages its scarce resources (Mankiw). Mankiw summarises its core ideas in ten principles in three groups.

A. How people make decisions (individual level)
  1. People face trade-offs — a student's time; efficiency vs equality.
  2. Opportunity cost — the cost of college includes wages forgone.
  3. Thinking at the margin — compare MB and MC; the airline seat example.
  4. Incentives — a petrol tax encourages fuel-efficient cars.
B. How people interact
  1. Trade can make everyone better off — specialisation and exchange.
  2. Markets usually organise activity well — the invisible hand.
  3. Governments can sometimes improve outcomes — market failure, equity.
C. How the economy as a whole works (societal level)
  1. Living standards depend on productivity — invest in education, capital, technology.
  2. Too much money printing causes inflation.
  3. Short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment — the Phillips curve.
D. How they guide decisions
  • Individuals: weigh opportunity costs, think at the margin, respond to incentives — in buying, studying, working, saving.
  • Society and government: design taxes and subsidies with incentives in mind; correct externalities; promote trade and productivity; keep money growth in check; balance inflation and unemployment.

The ten principles provide a toolkit that connects personal decisions to national policy.

Explain how the market system deals with risk. Discuss the different types of risks faced within a market economy.›10 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q20 · Univ 2024 Q3 (3m)

Every business faces the possibility that its future revenues will fall short of costs. A key strength of the market system is the way it allocates this risk to those best able and most willing to bear it.

A. How the market system deals with risk
  1. The profit system: owners invest their money and earn profits if the venture succeeds, or bear losses if it fails. The prospect of profit attracts risk-takers.
  2. Shielding employees and suppliers: workers receive fixed wages and suppliers fixed contract prices regardless of the firm's performance; business risk falls on owners.
  3. Benefits of restricting risk to owners: those most willing to bear risk do so; workers and suppliers can specialise without anxiety; it attracts capital and encourages innovation; failures are absorbed without wider collapse.
  4. Other risk-sharing tools: insurance, limited liability companies, diversification, contracts and futures markets.
B. Types of risk in a market economy
  1. Demand risk: changes in consumer tastes — feature phones replaced by smartphones.
  2. Competition risk: rivals offering better or cheaper products.
  3. Technological risk: innovations making products obsolete.
  4. Input-cost risk: rising fuel, raw-material or wage costs.
  5. Economic risk: recessions, inflation, interest-rate changes.
  6. Natural and political risk: floods, pandemics, changes in policy or regulation.

By concentrating risk on owners who receive profits as a reward, the market system encourages enterprise and investment while protecting workers and suppliers — a major reason for its success.
