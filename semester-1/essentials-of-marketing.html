10-minute cheat sheet

Every definition, list and framework that earns marks — scan this outside the hall.

MODULE IIntroduction to marketing

  • Marketing (Kotler): “the process by which companies create value for customers and build strong customer relationships in order to capture value from customers in return.”
  • AMA (2017): “the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large.”
  • Five concepts (in historical order): Production (make it cheap and available) → Product (make it better) → Selling (push it hard) → Marketing (find needs, satisfy them better than rivals) → Societal (satisfy needs and protect society's long-run welfare).
  • Selling vs marketing (Levitt): selling focuses on the seller's need to convert product into cash; marketing focuses on the buyer's need to be satisfied. Selling: factory → product → push → profit through volume. Marketing: market → needs → integrated marketing → profit through satisfaction.
  • Marketing environment: Micro = company, suppliers, intermediaries, customers, competitors, publics. Macro = PESTLE (political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental) + demographic.
  • Characteristics: customer-oriented · exchange process · human activity · continuous · goal-oriented (satisfaction + profit) · integrated · both art and science · dynamic.

MODULE IIProduct & pricing

  • Marketing mix (McCarthy 4Ps, 1960): Product, Price, Place, Promotion. Services add People, Process, Physical evidence → 7Ps (Booms & Bitner).
  • Consumer products: convenience (soap, bread) · shopping (clothes, TV — compared on price, quality, style) · specialty (Rolex — special effort) · unsought (insurance). Industrial: materials & parts · capital items · supplies & services. Services: intangible, inseparable, variable, perishable.
  • NPD (8 steps): idea generation → screening → concept development & testing → marketing strategy → business analysis → product development → test marketing → commercialisation.
  • PLC: Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline (profits peak in growth / early maturity).
  • Brand equity = the value a brand adds to a product. Aaker's elements: brand awareness, perceived quality, brand associations, brand loyalty, other proprietary assets. Positioning = the place a brand occupies in the consumer's mind relative to rivals.
  • Warranty = seller's written promise to repair/replace if the product fails within a period (conditions apply). Guarantee = broader assurance, often money-back, usually free of charge.
  • Pricing objectives: survival · profit maximisation / target return · market-share leadership · quality leadership · meeting competition · stabilising prices. Factors: demand, competition, costs, objectives, government regulation, product stage.
  • Pricing methods: Cost-based (cost-plus, markup, break-even/target return) · Value-based (price set on perceived value to the buyer) · Competition-based (going-rate, sealed-bid). Tactics: skimming, penetration, psychological (₹99), discount, bundle.

MODULE IIIDistribution & promotion

  • Channel of distribution: a set of interdependent organisations (intermediaries) that make a product available for use or consumption by the consumer.
  • Channel levels: zero-level (direct: M → C) · one-level (M → R → C) · two-level (M → W → R → C) · three-level (M → Agent → W → R → C).
  • Functions: information · promotion · contact · matching (sorting, grading, packing) · negotiation · physical distribution (transport, storage) · financing · risk-taking.
  • Promotion mix: advertising · sales promotion · personal selling · public relations · direct/digital marketing.
  • Sales promotion: short-term incentives to encourage purchase now. Consumer: samples, coupons, cash refunds, premiums, contests, price packs (buy 1 get 1), loyalty points, POP displays. Trade: allowances, dealer contests, free goods.
  • Personal selling: face-to-face, two-way presentation by the sales force to make sales and build relationships. Features: personal confrontation, cultivation, response, flexibility.
  • Public relations: building good relations with the company's publics — favourable publicity, good corporate image, handling unfavourable rumours. Tools: press releases, events, sponsorships, CSR, websites.

MODULE IVSustainable marketing

  • Sustainable marketing (Kotler): socially and environmentally responsible marketing that meets the present needs of consumers and businesses while preserving or enhancing the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
  • Pillars: the triple bottom line — People (social), Planet (environmental), Profit (economic).
  • Kotler's five principles: consumer-oriented · customer-value · innovative · sense-of-mission · societal marketing.
  • Waste hierarchy: Reduce → Reuse → Recycle → Recover → Dispose. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), circular economy.
  • Resource depletion → higher input costs, supply risk, regulation. Mitigation: efficiency, renewables, recycled inputs, eco-design, circular models, sustainable sourcing.
  • Energy consumption: energy used in production, transport, storage and product use. Cut it with efficient equipment, renewables, logistics optimisation, BEE star labels.
  • Green marketing: marketing products and practices on the basis of environmental benefits — eco-friendly product, green packaging, honest green claims, eco-labels. Avoid greenwashing. Examples: Tata Motors EVs, ITC paperboards, Body Shop.

Introduction to Marketing

★ Golden definition
“Marketing is the process by which companies create value for customers and build strong customer relationships in order to capture value from customers in return.”
— Philip Kotler & Gary Armstrong, Principles of Marketing. Shorter form for 3 marks: “marketing is managing profitable customer relationships.”

1.1Meaning, core concepts and characteristics

Marketing is wider than selling or advertising. It starts before production (finding out what customers need) and continues after the sale (service, relationship). The American Marketing Association (2017) defines it as “the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large.”

1.2The five marketing concepts and their evolution

A marketing concept (or philosophy) is the idea that guides a firm's marketing effort — whose interests come first: the factory's, the product's, the sales target's, the customer's, or society's. Historically, firms moved from the inside-out view to the outside-in view.

1.3Marketing vs selling — and the role of marketing in business

Theodore Levitt: “Selling focuses on the needs of the seller; marketing on the needs of the buyer.” Selling is one part of marketing; marketing is the whole process.

1.4The societal marketing concept

Societal marketing concept: the firm should determine the needs, wants and interests of target markets and deliver the desired satisfactions more effectively than competitors, in a way that maintains or improves the consumer's and society's well-being.

Why it emerged: the pure marketing concept can satisfy short-run wants while harming people in the long run — fast food and health, plastic bottles and pollution, tobacco.

  • Examples: Tata Tea Jaago Re (civic awareness), HUL Lifebuoy hand-washing campaign, ITC e-Choupal for farmers, reusable cloth bags in retail.
  • Benefits to firm: trust, goodwill, loyalty, fewer legal problems.

1.5Marketing environment and its impact on strategy

Marketing environment = the actors and forces outside marketing that affect management's ability to build and maintain successful relationships with target customers (Kotler). It brings opportunities and threats, so strategy must adapt to it.

★ Golden definition
“A product is anything that can be offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use, or consumption that might satisfy a want or need.”
— Kotler & Armstrong. It includes physical objects, services, events, persons, places, organisations and ideas.

2.1The marketing mix — 4Ps

Marketing mix = the set of controllable, tactical marketing tools that the firm blends to produce the response it wants in the target market. The term was coined by Neil Borden; the 4P framework is by E. Jerome McCarthy (1960).

PProduct

Variety, quality, design, features, brand name, packaging, services, warranties.

PPrice

List price, discounts, allowances, payment period, credit terms.

PPlace

Channels, coverage, locations, inventory, transportation, logistics.

PPromotion

Advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations, digital.

Services: 7Ps (Booms & Bitner, 1981)

4Ps + People (staff, customers) + Process (how service is delivered) + Physical evidence (ambience, uniforms, website).

Buyer's view: 4Cs (Lauterborn, 1990)

Product → Customer solution · Price → Customer cost · Place → Convenience · Promotion → Communication.

Exam angle: for “Elaborate the concept of marketing mix” (6m), give the definition → the 4Ps with 3–4 elements each → one example such as Maggi's mix → 7Ps in one line → importance (integration, customer satisfaction, competitive advantage).

2.3New product development (NPD) — process and strategies

A new product can be an original product, an improved or modified product, or a new brand the firm develops through its own R&D. Many new products fail, so firms follow a systematic eight-stage process.

NPD strategies in a competitive market
  • Innovation / first-mover — be first, build brand and patents (Apple iPhone, 2007)
  • Fast follower / imitation — improve on a pioneer's product at lower risk (“me-too”)
  • Line and brand extension — new variants under a known brand (Maggi Atta Noodles)
  • Product modification — improve quality, features or style of existing products
  • Acquisition / licensing — buy a firm, patent or licence to make someone else's product
  • Customer-centred & team-based NPD — co-create with customers; cross-functional teams for speed
Why new products fail
  • Overestimated market size
  • Poor design or wrong positioning
  • Wrong price, weak promotion
  • Higher-than-expected costs
  • Competitors fight back harder than expected

Example: Coca-Cola's “New Coke” (1985) was pulled after about three months.

2.5Brand, brand equity, positioning and differentiation

Brand (AMA): a name, term, sign, symbol or design, or a combination of these, that identifies the goods or services of one seller and differentiates them from competitors. Brand equity = the differential effect that knowing the brand name has on customer response to the product (Kotler) — the extra value a brand adds.

Building and managing brand equity
  • Consistent quality and a clear brand promise
  • Strong name, logo, tagline and packaging
  • Integrated communication and brand experiences
  • Loyalty programmes and good after-sales service
  • Careful brand extensions that don't dilute the brand
  • Track brand health — awareness, preference, loyalty
  • Keller's CBBE pyramid: identity → meaning → response → relationship (resonance)
Brand positioning

Positioning = arranging for a product to occupy a clear, distinctive and desirable place in the minds of target consumers relative to competing products (Ries & Trout popularised the term, 1972).

Positioning / differentiation basisExample

2.6Warranties and guarantees

Warranty
  • A written assurance by the seller that the product is free from defects and will perform as stated
  • If it fails within the warranty period, it will be repaired or replaced
  • Comes with conditions (proper use, no tampering); may be extended for a fee
  • Types: express (stated in writing) and implied (by law — fitness for purpose, merchantability)
  • Example: 1-year manufacturer's warranty on a Samsung TV; 2 years on panel
Guarantee
  • A broader promise, often about satisfaction or quality
  • Usually offers a refund or replacement, often with fewer conditions
  • Generally free; used as a confidence-building promotional tool
  • Example: “money-back guarantee within 7 days”; Domino's well-known “30 minutes or free” delivery promise
For buyers

Reduce perceived risk; protection against defects.

For sellers

Signal quality, build trust and repeat sales, differentiate the product.

Legal back-up

Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — “defect” and “deficiency in service” can be complained against.

2.7Pricing — objectives, policies and factors

Price = the amount of money charged for a product or service; the sum of the values customers exchange for the benefits of having or using it. It is the only element of the mix that produces revenue; all others are costs.

Pricing objectives
  1. Survival — cover variable costs in bad times
  2. Profit maximisation / target return on investment
  3. Market-share leadership — low prices to build volume
  4. Product-quality leadership — premium prices
  5. Meeting or preventing competition
  6. Price stabilisation — avoid price wars
  7. Social objectives — affordable essentials, e.g., Amul milk
Pricing policies
  • One-price vs variable price
  • Skimming — high initial price, then lower (new iPhones)
  • Penetration — low price to win share fast (Jio, 2016)
  • Psychological — ₹499, ₹99
  • Discounts & allowances — cash, quantity, seasonal, trade
  • Geographical — uniform delivered vs zone pricing
  • Product-line, bundle and promotional pricing
Internal factors
  • Marketing objectives
  • Costs — fixed and variable (the price floor)
  • Marketing-mix strategy and positioning
  • Product stage in the PLC
  • Organisational considerations
External factors
  • Demand — price elasticity, customer perceptions of value (the price ceiling)
  • Competition — rivals' prices, costs and reactions
  • Economic conditions — inflation, recession
  • Government regulation — GST, MRP rules, drug price control (DPCO)
  • Channel members' margins

Distribution and Promotion

Channels & intermediariesTypes of channelsSales promotionPersonal sellingPublic relations
★ Golden definition
“A marketing channel (or distribution channel) is a set of interdependent organisations that help make a product or service available for use or consumption by the consumer or business user.”
— Kotler & Armstrong. Place in the 4Ps = channels + physical distribution (logistics).

3.1Why intermediaries? The role of marketing channels

Role and importance of intermediaries
  • Contact efficiency — fewer transactions (diagram)
  • Bridge the gaps of time, place and possession between producer and consumer
  • Assortment — producers make large quantities of few items; consumers want small quantities of many
  • Market coverage — reach lakhs of retail outlets (HUL reaches millions of outlets through distributors)
  • Specialised skill — local knowledge, selling, storage
  • Financing and risk-bearing — buy stock, extend credit
  • Feedback — pass on market information to the producer

3.2Functions of distribution channels

Information

Gather and share data on customers, competitors and the environment.

Promotion

Spread persuasive communication about the offer — displays, demos.

Contact

Find and communicate with prospective buyers.

Matching

Shape the offer to buyers' needs — sorting, grading, assembling, packaging.

Negotiation

Agree on price and terms so ownership can transfer.

Physical distribution

Transport and store the goods.

Financing

Acquire and use funds to cover channel costs; give credit.

Risk-taking

Bear the risks of stock loss, price fall, bad debts.

Memory hook“I PC MN PFR” — the first five help complete transactions; the last three help fulfil them.

3.3Types of channels

Industrial channels

Manufacturer → industrial customer (direct, most common); or via industrial distributor, manufacturer's representative or sales branch.

Vertical marketing system (VMS)

Producer, wholesaler and retailer act as one system. Corporate (one owner — Bata, Raymond shops), contractual (franchise — McDonald's), administered (a dominant member leads — HUL).

Other types

Horizontal — two firms at the same level join (bank ATMs in petrol pumps). Multichannel / omnichannel — stores + app + website (Reliance Digital).

Factors in choosing a channelProduct (perishable, bulky, technical → short channel) · market (number and spread of buyers, order size) · company (finance, control wanted) · intermediaries (availability, cost, services) · environment (economy, law).

3.4Sales promotion — techniques and strategies

Sales promotion = short-term incentives to encourage the purchase or sale of a product or service. Advertising gives a reason to buy; sales promotion gives an incentive to buy now. Full detail on the Advertisement and Sales Promotion page.

Aimed atTechniques
Consumers (pull)Samples, coupons, cash refunds / rebates, price packs (“buy 1 get 1”, 20% extra), premiums (free gift), contests, sweepstakes and games, loyalty points, point-of-purchase displays, exchange offers
Trade (push)Buying allowances, display and advertising allowances, free goods, dealer contests, push money, trade shows
Sales forceBonuses, sales contests, incentive trips, recognition
Objectives
  • Induce trial of new products
  • Increase short-term sales, clear stock
  • Counter competitors' offers
  • Reward and retain loyal customers
  • Win shelf space and dealer support
Strategy and limitations
  • Set objectives → select tools → size of incentive, duration, conditions → pre-test → implement → evaluate
  • Overuse cheapens the brand image
  • Effects are short-lived; customers wait for deals

3.5Personal selling

Personal selling = personal presentation by the firm's sales force for the purpose of making sales and building customer relationships. It is the most effective promotion tool at later stages of buying (preference, conviction, action), but also the most expensive per contact.

Features
  • Personal, two-way communication — face-to-face, phone, video
  • Flexible — message adapted to each buyer
  • Immediate feedback and response; objections handled on the spot
  • Relationship building — long-term customer bonds (cultivation)
  • Persuasive — demonstrations, explanations
  • Costly — high cost per contact; needs trained staff
  • Best for technical, costly and industrial products, insurance

3.6Public relations (PR)

Public relations = building good relations with the company's various publics by obtaining favourable publicity, building a good corporate image, and handling or heading off unfavourable rumours, stories and events (Kotler).

Functions / tools
  • Press relations — news releases, press conferences
  • Product publicity — media coverage of products
  • Public affairs and lobbying — relations with government and the community
  • Investor relations — shareholders and the financial community
  • Events and sponsorships — sports, cultural events
  • Corporate identity — logo, stationery, uniforms
  • Public-service activities / CSR — e.g., Tata Trusts, Infosys Foundation
  • Crisis management
Importance in marketing
  • High credibility — news is believed more than ads
  • Low cost — no media space is bought
  • Builds corporate image and goodwill
  • Reaches people who avoid ads
  • Supports launches and brand building
  • Crisis management — e.g., Cadbury India's 2003 worm crisis: Project Vishwas, new tamper-proof packaging, and the Amitabh Bachchan campaign restored trust

★ Golden definition
“Sustainable marketing is socially and environmentally responsible marketing that meets the present needs of consumers and businesses while also preserving or enhancing the ability of future generations to meet their needs.”
— Kotler & Armstrong. It follows the Brundtland Report's (1987) idea of sustainable development.

4.1Meaning and pillars of sustainable marketing

  • People (social pillar): fair wages, safe products, health, community welfare, human rights in the supply chain.
  • Planet (environmental pillar): cut pollution, waste, carbon and water use; protect biodiversity.
  • Profit (economic pillar): the business must stay viable. Sustainability without profit cannot last, and profit without sustainability is short-sighted.
Kotler's five sustainable marketing principles
  1. Consumer-oriented — view activities from the consumer's point of view
  2. Customer-value — put resources into value-building investments
  3. Innovative — continuously seek real product and marketing improvements
  4. Sense-of-mission — define the mission in broad social terms
  5. Societal — balance consumer wants, company needs and society's long-run interests

4.2Marketing with sustainability concern

Critics argue that some marketing practices hurt consumers, society and other businesses. Sustainable marketing answers these criticisms.

Impact on consumers

High prices, deceptive practices, high-pressure selling, shoddy or unsafe products, planned obsolescence, poor service to disadvantaged consumers.

Impact on society

False wants and too much materialism, too few social goods, cultural pollution.

Impact on other businesses

Acquiring competitors, barriers to entry, predatory pricing.

How firms show sustainability concernEco-design and less packaging · ethical sourcing (Fairtrade) · honest communication (no greenwashing) · CSR (mandatory 2% of average net profit under Sec 135, Companies Act 2013, for qualifying companies) · consumer education · take-back and recycling schemes · sustainability reporting (SEBI's BRSR for the top 1,000 listed companies).

4.3Environmental impact management — waste and resource depletion

Environmental impact management = identifying, measuring and reducing the harmful effects of a firm's products and operations on the environment across the whole life cycle — raw material, production, distribution, use and disposal. Tools: life-cycle assessment (LCA), environmental impact assessment (EIA), ISO 14001 environmental management system.

Waste management strategies
  • Source reduction — design out waste, lightweight packaging
  • Circular economy — keep materials in use (repair, refurbish, recycle)
  • Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) — producers collect back plastic and e-waste (Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016; E-Waste Rules 2022)
  • Reverse logistics — take-back and exchange schemes
  • Zero-waste-to-landfill targets in factories
  • Consumer education — segregation at source, recycling labels
How resource depletion affects business
  • Rising input costs — water, minerals, fossil fuels
  • Supply disruption — shortages, price volatility
  • Stricter regulation — caps, taxes, bans
  • Reputation risk — consumers and investors punish wasteful firms
  • Threat to long-run survival of resource-based industries
Mitigation strategies
  • Efficiency — do more with less (lean, water recycling)
  • Substitution — renewable or recycled inputs
  • Eco-design — durable, repairable, recyclable products
  • Sustainable sourcing — certified wood, responsible agriculture
  • Circular business models — rental, sharing, refurbish
  • R&D and innovation, supplier collaboration, monitoring

4.4Energy consumption

Energy consumption = the amount of energy (electricity, fuel, heat) used by a firm in production, transport, storage, offices and stores, and by customers when they use its products. Most of it still comes from fossil fuels, so it drives cost and carbon emissions.

Why it matters to marketers

Energy cost affects price; buyers compare running costs (star ratings); carbon footprint affects brand image and regulation.

Reduce it

Energy audits; efficient machines and LED lighting; renewable power (rooftop solar); optimised logistics and routing; green buildings; ISO 50001 energy management.

Market it

BEE star labels (Bureau of Energy Efficiency, under the Energy Conservation Act 2001) on ACs and fridges; energy-saving claims (“5-star, saves ₹X a year”); EVs and inverter appliances.

4.5Green marketing — meaning, importance and strategies

Green marketing = the marketing of products that are presumed to be environmentally safe (AMA). It covers product modification, production process, packaging and advertising changes aimed at reducing environmental harm while satisfying customer needs. Also called environmental or ecological marketing.

PGreen product

Recyclable, energy-efficient, non-toxic, made from renewable materials.

PGreen price

Justify any premium with lower running cost, health or durability.

PGreen place

Low-emission logistics, local sourcing, reverse logistics.

PGreen promotion

Honest, specific claims; eco-labels; no greenwashing.

Importance
  • Protects the environment and conserves resources
  • Meets the demand of eco-conscious consumers
  • Competitive advantage and differentiation
  • Cost savings through efficiency and less waste
  • Compliance with environmental laws; access to green finance
  • Better brand image, goodwill and loyalty
  • Opens new markets — EVs, organic food, solar
Challenges
  • Higher initial cost of green technology and materials
  • Consumer scepticism; low willingness to pay a premium
  • Greenwashing — false or exaggerated green claims damage trust
  • Lack of standard definitions and certification
  • Results take time

Distinguish between skimming and penetration pricing.›3 marksLikely · not yet asked
  • Price skimming: a high initial price for a new product to “skim” maximum revenue from buyers willing to pay, then lower it over time. Suits innovative products with a strong image — new iPhone models.
  • Penetration pricing: a low initial price to enter the market quickly, attract many buyers and win a large share. Suits price-sensitive mass markets — Jio's free data in 2016.

Skimming builds profit per unit; penetration builds volume and market share.

What is cost-plus pricing?›3 marksLikely · not yet asked

Cost-plus (markup) pricing adds a standard markup to the cost of the product: Price = unit cost + markup %.

  • Example: unit cost ₹80, markup 25% → price ₹100.
  • Simple and seems fair, but ignores demand and competitors' prices.

Widely used by retailers, contractors and professionals.

What is a vertical marketing system?›3 marksLikely · not yet asked

A vertical marketing system (VMS) is a channel structure in which producers, wholesalers and retailers act as a unified system; one member owns the others, has contracts with them, or has so much power that they cooperate.

  • Corporate VMS — common ownership (Bata, Raymond's own shops).
  • Contractual VMS — franchises (McDonald's); administered VMS — led by a dominant firm (HUL).
What is the triple bottom line?›3 marksLikely · not yet asked

The triple bottom line (John Elkington, 1994) says a business should measure its performance on three dimensions, not only profit:

  • People — social responsibility to employees, communities, customers.
  • Planet — environmental responsibility.
  • Profit — economic value.

These are the three pillars of sustainable marketing.

What is greenwashing?›3 marksLikely · not yet asked

Greenwashing is making false, vague or exaggerated claims about the environmental benefits of a product or company to look green without real action.

  • Signs: vague words (“eco-friendly”, “natural”) without proof, hidden trade-offs, fake labels.
  • It destroys consumer trust; India's CCPA guidelines (2024) restrict misleading environmental claims.

Genuine green marketing uses specific, verifiable claims and recognised eco-labels.

Elaborate the evolution of marketing concepts.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q11 · Amal 2025 Q2 (3m)

A marketing concept is the philosophy that guides a firm's marketing effort. Over time, firms moved from a factory-centred (inside-out) view to a customer- and society-centred (outside-in) view.

  1. Production concept (till ~1920s): consumers want products that are available and cheap; focus on mass production and distribution efficiency — Ford's Model T.
  2. Product concept: consumers want quality and features; focus on product improvement. Risk: marketing myopia.
  3. Selling concept (~1930s–50s): after the Depression, supply exceeded demand; firms believed consumers would not buy enough unless pushed by aggressive selling and promotion.
  4. Marketing concept (~1950s on): find out what customers need and satisfy them better than rivals; customer focus, integrated marketing, profit through satisfaction.
  5. Societal marketing concept (~1970s on): satisfy wants while protecting consumers' and society's long-run welfare — response to pollution, consumerism and resource shortages.
  6. Today — holistic and sustainable marketing: relationship, digital and sustainable marketing extend the societal idea.

Marketing has evolved from “selling what we make” to “making what customers need — responsibly”.

Explain the features of marketing concepts.›6 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q11

The (modern) marketing concept holds that achieving organisational goals depends on knowing the needs and wants of target markets and delivering the desired satisfactions better than competitors. Its main features:

  1. Customer orientation: the customer is the starting point — “customer is king”.
  2. Target market focus: the firm selects segments it can serve best instead of trying to serve everyone.
  3. Integrated marketing: all 4Ps and all departments (production, finance, HR) work together to serve customers.
  4. Profit through customer satisfaction: profit is the reward for satisfying customers, not for sales volume alone.
  5. Research-based decisions: marketing research finds needs before production.
  6. Long-term relationships: focus on customer retention and loyalty, not one-off sales.
  7. Outside-in perspective: starts with the market and works back to the factory.
  8. Social responsibility (societal extension): concern for consumer and social welfare.

Under the marketing concept the firm does not find the right customers for its products; it finds the right products for its customers.

Describe the important characteristics of marketing.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Marketing is the process of creating, communicating and delivering value to customers and managing customer relationships profitably (Kotler). Its characteristics:

  1. Customer-oriented: begins with customer needs and ends with their satisfaction.
  2. Exchange process: goods and services are exchanged for money or other value.
  3. Continuous activity: research, product, pricing, distribution, promotion and after-sales service go on in a cycle.

Elaborate the concept of marketing mix.›6 marks

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q13

The marketing mix is the set of controllable, tactical marketing tools — product, price, place and promotion — that the firm blends to produce the response it wants in the target market. Borden coined the term; McCarthy grouped it into the 4Ps (1960).

The 4Ps
  1. Product: the goods-and-services combination offered — variety, quality, design, features, brand, packaging, warranty.
  2. Price: the money customers pay — list price, discounts, allowances, credit terms.
  3. Place: activities that make the product available — channels, coverage, inventory, transport.
  4. Promotion: communicating merits and persuading buyers — advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, PR.
Extensions
  • 7Ps for services (Booms & Bitner): + People, Process, Physical evidence.
  • 4Cs (Lauterborn): customer solution, customer cost, convenience, communication.

Example — Maggi: product = 2-minute noodles in many flavours; price = ₹14 small pack; place = every kirana and supermarket; promotion = TV ads aimed at mothers and children.

A good mix is consistent and integrated — every P supports the same positioning.

Explain the different methods of pricing with examples.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q17 · Univ 2024 Q19 (10m)

A pricing method is the procedure used to arrive at a price. Product costs set the floor, customers' perceived value sets the ceiling, and competitors' prices set the range in between (Kotler).

A. Cost-based
  1. Cost-plus / markup: price = unit cost + standard markup. Cost ₹80, markup 25% → ₹100.
  2. Break-even / target-return: price set to break even or earn a target return. BEP units = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost per unit).
B. Value-based
  1. Perceived-value pricing: price set on what buyers think the product is worth — luxury brands.
  2. Good-value / EDLP: quality at a fair price every day — D-Mart.
C. Competition-based
  1. Going-rate: follow the market price — petrol, steel.
  2. Sealed-bid: price set on expected rival bids — government tenders.

Strategies used with these: skimming, penetration, psychological (₹999), discount and bundle pricing.

No single method suits all firms; most combine cost, value and competition considerations.

Give practical strategies adopted in the different stages of the PLC of a product.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q18 · Amal 2025 Q8 (3m)

The product life cycle has four stages; each needs a different marketing strategy. (Draw the PLC curve from Module II, 2.4.)

1. Introduction
  • Build awareness and trial: free samples, launch advertising, introductory offers.
  • Skimming (premium tech) or penetration (FMCG) pricing; selective distribution; basic product.
2. Growth
  • Improve quality; add features, models and warranties.
  • Enter new segments and channels — intensive distribution; lower prices to attract price-sensitive buyers.
  • Shift advertising from awareness to preference.
3. Maturity
  • Modify the market: new users and uses (Dettol from first-aid to floors).
  • Modify the product: new variants, packaging, features.
  • Modify the mix: price cuts, sales promotion, new channels (online).

  • Maintain (hoping rivals exit), harvest (cut costs, milk the brand), or drop the product.
  • Possible repositioning or revival — e.g., nostalgic relaunches.

Knowing the stage helps a firm choose the right mix and extend the product's profitable life.

Analyse the role of distribution channels in distributing products. / What are the functions of distribution channels?›6 marks★ Asked 3×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q15 · Amal 2025 Q13 · Univ 2024 Q6 (3m)

A distribution channel is the set of interdependent organisations — agents, wholesalers, retailers — that make a product available to consumers. Channels bridge the gaps of time, place and possession between producer and consumer.

Role (why channels are needed)
  • Contact efficiency: 3 producers × 3 customers need 9 contacts; through one distributor, only 6.
  • Assortment: producers make large quantities of a few items; consumers want small quantities of many.
  • Wide market coverage, local knowledge and feedback.
Functions (Kotler)
  1. Information — collect and share market data.
  2. Promotion — persuasive communication, displays.
  3. Contact — find and reach prospective buyers.
  4. Matching — sorting, grading, assembling, packaging to suit buyers.
  5. Negotiation — agree on price and terms.
  6. Physical distribution — transport and storage.
  7. Financing — stock holding and credit to retailers.
  8. Risk-taking — bear the risk of loss, price fall, bad debts.

Intermediaries perform these functions more efficiently than producers could alone, cutting cost and widening reach.

  5. Persuasion and demonstration: the product can be shown and explained.
  6. Handling objections and closing the sale on the spot.
  7. Costly: the most expensive promotion tool per contact; needs trained salespeople.
  8. Best for technical, high-value and industrial products, and the later stages of buying (conviction, purchase).

Process: prospecting → pre-approach → approach → presentation → handling objections → closing → follow-up.

Personal selling is the most persuasive and relationship-oriented element of the promotion mix.

Discuss the importance of public relations in marketing.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q14 · Univ 2024 Q8 (3m)

Public relations is building good relations with the company's publics through favourable publicity, a good corporate image and handling unfavourable events (Kotler).

  1. High credibility: news stories and features are believed more than paid ads.
  2. Low cost: media space is not bought, so reach is cheap.
  3. Builds corporate image and goodwill with customers, investors, government and community.
  4. Supports product launches through press coverage, events and influencers.
  5. Reaches people who avoid advertising.
  6. Crisis management: protects reputation in bad times. Cadbury India's 2003 worm crisis was handled through Project Vishwas, tamper-proof packaging and an Amitabh Bachchan campaign.
  7. Investor and government relations: lobbying and financial communication.
  8. CSR communication: showcases social work (Tata, Infosys Foundation).

PR complements advertising — advertising buys attention; PR earns trust.

How does resource depletion affect businesses, and what strategies can companies adopt to mitigate this issue?›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q17 · Amal 2025 Q20 (10m)

Resource depletion is the using up of natural resources — water, minerals, forests, fossil fuels — faster than they can be replaced.

Effects on business
  1. Rising input costs and price volatility.
  2. Supply disruption and shortages (e.g., water shortages hitting beverage plants).
  3. Stricter regulation — caps, taxes, bans, EPR obligations.
  4. Reputation and investor pressure on wasteful firms.
  5. Threat to long-term survival of resource-dependent industries.
Mitigation strategies
  1. Resource efficiency — lean production, water recycling, less material.
  2. Substitution — renewable and recycled inputs (recycled PET, solar power).
  3. Eco-design — durable, repairable, recyclable products.
  4. Circular business models — take-back, refurbish, rent, share.
  5. Sustainable sourcing and supplier partnerships; R&D for alternatives.

Firms that cut resource dependence lower costs and risks and gain a sustainability edge.

  2. Meets growing green demand — eco-conscious consumers, especially the young.

  4. Cost savings — energy, water and material efficiency.

Describe the pillars of sustainable marketing.›6 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q16 · Univ 2024 Q9 (3m)

Sustainable marketing meets present needs while preserving the ability of future generations to meet theirs. It rests on three pillars — the triple bottom line (John Elkington, 1994). (Draw the three-circle diagram from Module IV, 4.1.)

1. People — the social pillar
  • Fair wages and safe working conditions; no child labour in the supply chain.
  • Safe, honest products; consumer health and welfare; community development and CSR.
2. Planet — the environmental pillar
  • Cut emissions, waste, water and energy use; eco-friendly packaging.
  • Protect biodiversity; responsible sourcing and recycling.
3. Profit — the economic pillar
  • The business must remain financially viable to continue doing good.
  • Long-term value instead of short-term gain; efficient use of resources.

Supporting principles (Kotler): consumer-oriented, customer-value, innovative, sense-of-mission and societal marketing.

Sustainable marketing succeeds only where all three pillars overlap.

Explain the marketing environment and its impact on business strategy.›6 marksLikely · not yet asked

The marketing environment is the set of actors and forces outside marketing that affect a firm's ability to serve its customers (Kotler). It creates opportunities and threats, so strategy must adapt.

Micro environment

The company, suppliers, marketing intermediaries, customers, competitors and publics — close to the firm and partly influenceable.

Macro environment (PESTLE + demographic)
  1. Political-legal: GST, plastic ban, consumer laws → new pricing and packaging.
  2. Economic: incomes, inflation, interest rates → price and product decisions.
  3. Social-cultural: health awareness → sugar-free and millet products.
  4. Technological: UPI, e-commerce, AI → digital marketing, D2C brands.
  5. Natural: climate change, resource scarcity → green products.
  6. Demographic: a young, urbanising population → youth-focused brands.

Firms scan the environment (SWOT/PESTLE) to seize opportunities and defend against threats.

Explain the factors determining the price of a product.›6 marksLikely · not yet asked

Price lies between the floor set by costs and the ceiling set by customer value; many internal and external factors decide where.

Internal factors
  1. Marketing objectives — survival, profit, market share, quality leadership.
  2. Costs — fixed and variable; the price floor.
  3. Marketing-mix strategy — price must fit product, place and promotion decisions.
  4. Stage in the PLC and organisational considerations.
External factors
  1. Demand — price elasticity and perceived value; the price ceiling.
  2. Competition — rivals' prices, costs and likely reactions.
  3. Economic conditions — inflation, recession, incomes.
  4. Government regulation — GST, MRP rules, drug price control.
  5. Channel members — margins expected by dealers.

Good pricing balances all these factors with the firm's objectives.

Explain the different types of distribution channels.›6 marksLikely · not yet asked

Channels are classified by the number of intermediary levels and by how members are organised.

By levels (consumer goods)
  1. Zero-level (direct): Manufacturer → Consumer — company website, door-to-door (Eureka Forbes).
  2. One-level: M → Retailer → C — cars through dealers.
  3. Two-level: M → Wholesaler → Retailer → C — most FMCG goods.
  4. Three-level: M → Agent → Wholesaler → Retailer → C — agricultural and imported goods.
Industrial channels

Direct to industrial users, or through industrial distributors, manufacturer's representatives or sales branches.

By organisation
  • Conventional — independent members.
  • Vertical (VMS) — corporate, contractual (franchise), administered.
  • Horizontal — firms at one level join forces.
  • Multichannel / omnichannel — stores, app and website together.

The right channel depends on the product, market, company resources, intermediaries and environment.

What is sales promotion? Explain consumer and trade promotion techniques.›6 marksLikely · not yet asked

Sales promotion consists of short-term incentives to encourage the purchase or sale of a product now (Kotler).

Consumer promotions (pull)
  1. Samples — free trial quantity.
  2. Coupons — savings certificates.
  3. Cash refunds / rebates.
  4. Price packs — “buy 1 get 1 free”, “20% extra”.
  5. Premiums — free gift with purchase.
  6. Contests, sweepstakes and games.

Explain the waste management strategies of a sustainable business.›6 marksLikely · not yet asked

Waste management is the collection, reduction, reuse, recycling and safe disposal of waste. Sustainable firms follow the waste hierarchy: Reduce → Reuse → Recycle → Recover → Dispose.

  1. Source reduction: design out waste; lighter, minimal packaging.
  2. Reuse: refill packs, returnable bottles and crates.
  3. Recycling: recycled inputs; recycling labels for consumers.
  4. Recovery: waste-to-energy, composting organic waste.
  5. Extended Producer Responsibility: collect back plastic and e-waste (Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016; E-Waste Rules 2022).
  6. Reverse logistics and exchange offers.
  7. Zero-waste-to-landfill targets and waste audits.

Good waste management lowers cost, meets the law and strengthens a green brand image.

What is pricing? Explain in detail the various methods of pricing.›10 marks★ Asked 2×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q19 · Amal 2025 Q17 (6m)

Price is the amount of money charged for a product or service — the sum of the values customers exchange for the benefits of having or using it. Pricing is the process of deciding that amount. Price is the only element of the marketing mix that produces revenue; all the others are costs.

A. Pricing objectives and factors (brief)
  1. Objectives: survival, profit maximisation, target return, market-share leadership, quality leadership, meeting competition, price stability.
  2. Factors: internal — costs, objectives, marketing mix, PLC stage; external — demand and perceived value, competition, economic conditions, government regulation (GST, MRP, DPCO).
  3. The three anchors: costs set the floor, customer value sets the ceiling, competitors' prices set the range in between.
B. Cost-based methods
  1. Cost-plus / markup pricing: price = unit cost + standard markup. Unit cost ₹80 + 25% → ₹100. Simple and fair-seeming, but ignores demand and competition.
  2. Break-even and target-return pricing: BEP (units) = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). With FC ₹2,00,000, VC ₹60 and price ₹100, BEP = 5,000 units. Shows the volume needed at each price.
  3. Marginal-cost pricing: price covers variable cost plus some contribution — useful for spare capacity or export orders.
C. Value-based methods
  1. Perceived-value pricing: price set on buyers' perception of value; the product and costs are designed to fit the target price — premium brands, branded medicines.
  2. Good-value pricing / EDLP: the right quality at a fair price every day — D-Mart.
  3. Value-added pricing: add features and services to justify higher prices instead of cutting price.
D. Competition-based methods
  1. Going-rate pricing: price at, above or below the leader — petrol, steel, cement.
  2. Sealed-bid (tender) pricing: price set on expected rival bids — government contracts.
E. Pricing strategies used alongside

Skimming (new iPhones) · penetration (Jio, 2016) · psychological (₹999) · discounts and allowances · product-line and bundle pricing · geographical pricing · dynamic pricing (airlines, Uber).

No single method is best. Sound pricing starts from customer value, checks costs for a floor, and positions the price against competitors to meet the firm's objectives.

Analyse the concept of green marketing. What strategies can businesses implement to effectively promote environmentally friendly products and practices while maintaining profitability?›10 marks★ Asked 3×

Asked in: Univ 2024 Q20 · Univ 2024 Q18 (6m) · Amal 2025 Q7 (3m)

Green marketing is the marketing of products that are presumed to be environmentally safe (AMA). It covers eco-friendly product design, cleaner production, green packaging, responsible distribution and honest environmental communication. It is a practical application of the societal and sustainable marketing concepts.

A. The concept analysed
  1. Why it arose: climate change, pollution, resource depletion, stricter laws and eco-aware consumers.
  2. Green 4Ps: green product (recyclable, energy-efficient), green price (justified by value), green place (low-emission logistics), green promotion (verifiable claims, eco-labels such as Ecomark).
  3. Benefits: environmental protection, differentiation, cost savings, compliance, brand loyalty, new markets.
  4. Challenges: higher initial costs, consumer scepticism, low willingness to pay a premium, greenwashing risk, lack of standards.
B. Strategies to promote green products and practices
  1. Eco-design and life-cycle thinking: design for durability, repair and recycling; use LCA to cut impact at every stage.
  2. Green packaging: less material, recycled or compostable packs, refill stations.
  3. Credible communication: specific, verifiable claims (“30% less water”), third-party certification, eco-labels; avoid greenwashing (CCPA greenwashing guidelines, 2024).
  4. Consumer education and engagement: explain benefits — savings, health — not only the environment; take-back and recycling drives.
  5. Green supply chain: sustainable sourcing, local suppliers, electric or optimised logistics, reverse logistics.
  6. Partnerships and cause marketing: NGOs, governments, tree-planting or clean-up campaigns.
  7. Choose the right strategy level (Ginsberg & Bloom matrix): lean, defensive, shaded or extreme green, depending on the size of the green segment and the ability to differentiate.
C. Maintaining profitability
  1. Cost savings: energy, water and material efficiency pay back investment (LEDs, solar).
  2. Premium pricing where value is real: lower running costs (EVs, 5-star appliances), health benefits (organic food).
  3. Market expansion and loyalty: new eco-segments; loyal, less price-sensitive customers.
  4. Risk reduction and finance: fewer penalties, access to green bonds and ESG investors; government incentives (FAME/PM E-DRIVE for EVs).

Discuss the process of new product development and strategies in the competitive market.›10 marks

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q19

A new product is an original product, product improvement, product modification or new brand developed through the firm's own R&D (Kotler). New products are vital for growth, but most fail, so firms follow a systematic process and a clear strategy.

A. The NPD process (draw the eight-box flow)
  1. Idea generation: internal R&D and employees; customers, competitors, dealers, suppliers; crowdsourcing.
  2. Idea screening: drop poor ideas early using criteria — market size, fit with company, cost. Avoid drop-errors and go-errors.
  3. Concept development and testing: turn the idea into a detailed consumer concept; test with target buyers.
  4. Marketing strategy development: target market, positioning, sales and profit goals; planned price, distribution and budget; long-run goals.
  5. Business analysis: estimate demand, costs and profits to see if objectives are met.
  6. Product development: build prototypes; functional and consumer testing.
  7. Test marketing: try the product and programme in a realistic market setting (a few cities).
  8. Commercialisation: full launch — decide when, where, to whom and how.
B. NPD strategies in a competitive market
  1. First-mover / innovation strategy: be first, build brand leadership and patents — Apple iPhone.
  2. Fast-follower / imitation: enter after the pioneer with an improved or cheaper version — lower risk.
  3. Line and brand extension: new variants under a strong brand — Maggi Atta Noodles, Amul ice creams.
  4. Product modification and improvement: new features, quality, design.
  5. Acquisition and licensing: buy companies, patents or licences.
  6. Customer-centred and team-based NPD: co-create with customers; cross-functional teams cut time to market.
  7. Systematic innovation management: an idea manager, idea portals and stage-gate reviews.
C. Why new products fail and how to avoid it

Overestimated market, poor design, wrong positioning, wrong price, weak launch promotion, higher costs, competitors' retaliation. New Coke (1985) was withdrawn within months. Remedies: research, test marketing and a strong launch plan.

A disciplined NPD process combined with the right competitive strategy turns ideas into profitable products and keeps a firm ahead of rivals.

Critically examine environmental impact management in the context of sustainable marketing.›10 marks★ Asked 3×

Asked in: Amal 2025 Q20 · Univ 2024 Q17 (6m) · Univ 2024 Q10 (3m)

Environmental impact management (EIM) is the process of identifying, measuring and reducing the harmful effects of a firm's products and operations on the environment across the whole life cycle — sourcing, production, distribution, use and disposal. In sustainable marketing it is the “Planet” pillar put into practice.

A. Components of EIM
  1. Assessment tools: Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) for projects; Life-Cycle Assessment (LCA) for products; carbon and water footprints; ISO 14001 environmental management systems.
  2. Waste management: the hierarchy reduce → reuse → recycle → recover → dispose; EPR under the Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016 and E-Waste Rules 2022; reverse logistics.
  3. Resource depletion mitigation: efficiency, renewable and recycled inputs, eco-design, circular business models, sustainable sourcing.
  4. Energy consumption management: energy audits, efficient equipment, renewables, ISO 50001, BEE star-labelled products.
  5. Pollution control: emission and effluent treatment, cleaner technology.
  6. Green marketing: communicating real improvements to customers through credible claims and eco-labels.
B. Role in sustainable marketing — the case for
  1. Meets present needs without harming future generations.
  2. Cuts costs, reduces regulatory and supply risk.
  3. Builds brand trust and loyalty; attracts ESG investors; supports BRSR reporting.
  4. Creates new markets — EVs, solar, recycled products.
C. Critical examination — limitations
  1. High cost of clean technology; hard for small firms.
  2. Measurement problems: LCA and carbon data are complex and often estimated.
  3. Greenwashing: firms may publicise small actions while the core business stays polluting.
  4. Rebound effect: efficient products can increase total consumption.
  5. Weak enforcement and consumer unwillingness to pay more.

EIM is essential to sustainable marketing, but it works only when it is measured honestly, built into strategy and backed by regulation and informed consumers — otherwise it becomes mere image-building.

💡 Examiner's note“Critically examine” means both sides — the benefits and the limitations. Cover the three syllabus units (waste, resource depletion, energy) as sub-points, and finish with a balanced judgement.
