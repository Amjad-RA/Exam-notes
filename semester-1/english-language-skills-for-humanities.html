💡 What the three papers tell you
  • Your college paper follows the syllabus weights; the university papers do not. Amal College gave Modules III and IV 16 marks each — the university gave Module IV nothing. Prepare all four modules evenly.
  • “Anthem” and “Rip it Out” were asked in all three papers. Treat them as certain.
  • Section A is never from the syllabus texts. It is an unseen passage — caffeine science (2024), Tagore on nationalism (2025), girls' education and Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (Amal 2025). Comprehension technique is the only preparation — see Section 3.
  • Ambedkar's speech is the only text never asked. It carries Module IV weight, so expect it next.
  • Questions repeat by text, not by wording: Anthem was asked as “religious themes”, “central messages” and “central themes”; Rip it Out as “individual vs society”, “effective speaker” and “humour”. Know each text from several angles.

10-minute cheat sheet

Author · year · form · one-line gist · the quotes to drop · the themes. Twelve texts, scannable outside the hall.

MODULE IListening

  • I Have a Dream — Martin Luther King Jr. · speech · 28 Aug 1963, Lincoln Memorial, March on Washington. America's “promissory note” returned as a “bad check” marked “insufficient funds”. Non-violent “soul force”; children “judged … by the content of their character”. Devices: anaphora, metaphor, Biblical allusion. → Civil Rights Act 1964, Voting Rights Act 1965.
  • Anthem — Leonard Cohen (Canadian, 1934–2016) · song · album The Future, 1992. Refrain: “There is a crack in everything / That's how the light gets in.” Themes: imperfection as grace, hope, living in the present, hypocrisy of power, peace “bought and sold”.
  • Art as Social Work — Amy Morales Lara · TEDxCUNY talk (2023). Bronx-born daughter of immigrants, training in social work at CUNY Hunter College. Art + social work → curiosity about people and places, being a supportive helper, voice and healing for marginalised communities.

MODULE IISpeaking

  • On Soft Language — George Carlin (US, 1937–2008) · stand-up, HBO special Doin' It Again (1990). Euphemisms are “words that hide the truth”. Shell shock → battle fatigue → operational exhaustion → post-traumatic stress disorder: “the pain is completely buried under jargon”. Also toilet paper → bathroom tissue, sneakers → running shoes, dump → landfill, slums → substandard housing, old → senior citizens, die → pass away. Stand-up = social criticism: laughter lowers defences.
  • Death of a Salesman (Act I, opening scene) — Arthur Miller · play, 1949, Pulitzer Prize. Willy Loman, 60-plus salesman, returns home “tired to the death”; Linda soothes him; Biff (34) “lost”; Swiss cheese; boxed-in by apartments; the 1928 Chevvy. Themes: American Dream, illusion vs reality, family, ageing.
  • “Rip it Out” — Dead Poets Society (1989), dir. Peter Weir; Robin Williams as John Keating, Welton Academy, 1959. Pritchard's graph of poetry → “Excrement… Rip it out!”; “a battle, a war”; “think for yourselves”; Whitman → “What will your verse be?”

MODULE IIIReading

  • The Second Coming — W. B. Yeats (Irish, Nobel 1923) · poem, written 1919, published 1920. “Turning and turning in the widening gyre / The falcon cannot hear the falconer; / Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold.” “The best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity.” A sphinx-like “rough beast … slouches towards Bethlehem”. Post-WWI chaos; cyclical history (gyres).
  • The Frog Prince — James Finn Garner, Politically Correct Bedtime Stories (1994) · satirical retelling. The frog is a swindling property developer punished by a spell; restored, he wants to develop more land; the princess rejects the “male power structures in the castle” (marriage, property, patriarchy) and ends his schemes by killing him → the eco-feminist reversal of the fairy tale.
  • Optimism, Part I “Optimism Within” — Helen Keller (1880–1968) · essay, 1903. Deaf-blind from 19 months; freed by “a little word from the fingers of another”. “Darkness cannot shut me in again. I have had a glimpse of the shore.” “The struggle which evil necessitates is one of the greatest blessings.” Optimism without knowing evil is “a house builded on sand”. Rejects the “dangerous optimism of ignorance and indifference”; believes in “the preponderance of good”.

MODULE IVWriting

  • Annihilation of Caste, Section 4 — Dr B. R. Ambedkar · speech prepared for the 1936 Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal conference, Lahore (cancelled; self-published). Caste is “not merely division of labour… also a division of labourers”: graded hierarchy, not aptitude, blocks job mobility → unemployment, no choice → “neither men's hearts nor their minds are in their work”.
  • The World-Renowned Nose — Vaikom Muhammad Basheer (“Beypore Sultan”, 1908–1994) · Malayalam satire Vishwavikhyathamaya Mookku, 1954. Mookken the illiterate cook's nose grows “level with his navel” → fame, money, films → parties fight over him → “rubber nose” scandal → experts from 48 countries prick it → “Mookashri”, MP. Satire on mass psychology, media, politics.
  • For the Love of Cinema — review of Cinema Paradiso (1988), dir. Giuseppe Tornatore. Film director Salvatore (Totò) returns to his Sicilian village for projectionist Alfredo's funeral; censored kisses, fire, lost love Elena; Alfredo's reel of kisses. Ennio Morricone score; Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film. A model of review writing.

Module I · 7 hours · 10 marks

Listening

I Have a DreamAnthemArt as Social Work
★ Golden definition
“Listening is the process of receiving, constructing meaning from, and responding to spoken and/or nonverbal messages.”
— International Listening Association. Five stages (DeVito): receiving → understanding → remembering → evaluating → responding.

1.1I Have a Dream — Martin Luther King Jr.

Speech28 August 1963Lincoln Memorial, Washington D.C.March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom · ~250,000 people

A hundred years after the Emancipation Proclamation, Black Americans are still not free; King demands justice now, through non-violence, and paints a dream of a united, colour-blind America.

Themes
  • Racial equality and justice
  • Freedom and the unfulfilled American Dream
  • Non-violent resistance
  • Hope and faith
  • Brotherhood across races
Devices
  • Anaphora: “I have a dream”, “Now is the time”, “Let freedom ring”
  • Metaphor: bad check, “table of brotherhood”, “oasis of freedom”
  • Allusion: Lincoln, Declaration of Independence, the Bible (Amos, Isaiah)
Quote bank
  • “America has given the Negro people a bad check, a check which has come back marked ‘insufficient funds.’”
  • “a lonely island of poverty in the midst of a vast ocean of material prosperity”
  • “meeting physical force with soul force”
  • “…not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character.”
  • “Free at last! Free at last! Thank God Almighty, we are free at last!”
Asked: conditions that led to the speech (10m, 2024) · relevance today (6m, 2025). Likely: rhetorical devices; the bad-check metaphor.

1.2Anthem — Leonard Cohen

Song / lyric poemAlbum The Future, 1992Canadian poet-singer, 1934–2016

The world is broken — wars, hypocrisy, peace for sale — but brokenness is where hope enters: “There is a crack in everything / That's how the light gets in.”

Image / symbolMeaning
“Ring the bells that still can ring”Use whatever voice, faith or joy you still have; church bells = prayer, celebration
“Forget your perfect offering”Stop waiting to be flawless; God and life accept imperfect gifts
The crack → the lightFlaws and wounds are openings for grace, truth and growth (paradox)
Birds: “Start again”Renewal; don't dwell on the past or the future — live now
Holy dove “bought and sold”Peace and the Holy Spirit commodified; “the dove is never free”
“killers in high places say their prayers out loud”Religious and political hypocrisy
Love comes “like a refugee”Love survives, but battered and displaced
Central themes
  • Acceptance of imperfection
  • Hope amid despair
  • Spirituality without perfection
  • Living in the present
  • Critique of war, power and hypocrisy
  • Resilient love
Devices
  • Refrain — the four-line chorus repeats like a hymn
  • Paradox — the crack lets in light
  • Symbolism — bells, dove, birds
  • Simile — “like a refugee”
  • Religious diction — offering, holy, prayers, signs
Asked in all three papers: religious and spiritual themes (2024) · central messages (Univ 2025) · central themes (Amal 2025).

TEDx talkRecorded 2023Bronx-born daughter of immigrant parentsMSW student, Silberman School of Social Work, Hunter College

Art and social work belong together: both teach curiosity about the possibilities in people and places, and both let a “helper” walk alongside a community rather than above it.

TED talkFormal speech (e.g., MLK)
LengthShort — 18 minutes maximumSet by the occasion
AimOne “idea worth spreading”Persuade, inspire, mark an event
StyleConversational, personal story, visualsFormal, rhetorical, oratorical
Before the examNo public transcript of this talk exists. Re-watch it from the syllabus link and note one personal story she tells — a specific reference lifts any answer on this text.

Module II · 7 hours · 10 marks

Speaking

George Carlin on Soft LanguageDeath of a Salesman“Rip it Out” — Dead Poets Society
★ Golden definition
“Speaking is the process of building and sharing meaning through the use of verbal and non-verbal symbols, in a variety of contexts.”
— Chaney (1998). Effective speaking combines content, delivery (voice, pace, pause), body language and audience awareness — Aristotle's ethos, pathos, logos.

2.1On Soft Language — George Carlin

Stand-up comedyHBO special Doin' It Again, 1990American comedian and social critic, 1937–2008

Euphemisms are “words that hide the truth”: Americans invent soft language to avoid reality, and every generation buries pain deeper under jargon.

Honest wordSoft language
toilet paperbathroom tissue
sneakersrunning shoes
the dumpthe landfill
poor people live in slumsthe economically disadvantaged occupy substandard housing
old peoplesenior citizens
people diepeople pass away
Why Carlin objects
  • Conceals reality and suffering
  • Lets the comfortable avoid guilt
  • Denies victims attention and help
  • Lets governments and corporations manipulate (doublespeak)
Stand-up as social criticism
  • Laughter lowers defences
  • Exaggeration exposes absurdity
  • The comic can say what others won't
Asked: stand-up as social criticism (6m, 2024) · Carlin's examples of soft language (6m, Amal 2025).

2.2Death of a Salesman — Arthur Miller (Act I, opening scene)

Play · tragedy1949 · Pulitzer PrizeBrooklyn, New YorkExcerpt ends: Linda “takes his jacket, and goes out of the bedroom”

An exhausted, ageing salesman comes home in the middle of the night, unable to drive any further, and his loving wife tries to hold together a man breaking under the weight of his dreams.

Willy Loman
  • 60-plus travelling salesman — “Low-man”
  • Exhausted, mentally slipping into the past
  • Proud yet insecure (“I'm vital in New England”)
  • Self-contradictory and irritable (“Why am I always being contradicted?”)
  • Blames others — Howard, the city, Biff
  • Loving but lost in illusions of success
Linda and the family
  • Linda: patient, protective, calls him “dear”, manages his moods
  • Biff (34): drifting farmhand; the son Willy's dreams rest on
  • Happy: younger son, sleeping upstairs
  • Howard Wagner: the boss's son who “don't appreciate”
  • House: “Work a lifetime to pay off a house… and there's nobody to live in it.”
Themes

The American Dream and its failure · illusion vs reality · past vs present · family and fatherhood · ageing and obsolescence · urban crowding. Miller called this kind of play the tragedy of the common man.

Asked: character of Willy Loman (6m, 2024) · family and relationships (10m, 2025).

2.3“Rip it Out” — Dead Poets Society

Film scene1989 · dir. Peter WeirRobin Williams as John KeatingWelton Academy, 1959 · “Tradition, Honor, Discipline, Excellence”

A new English teacher makes his class tear out a textbook essay that measures poetry with a graph, telling them to think for themselves, because poetry is what we stay alive for.

Pritchard / Welton
  • Measure poetry like plumbing
  • Obedience, tradition
  • Career: medicine, law, business
  • Conformity
Keating
  • “We're not laying pipe”
  • “Think for yourselves”
  • “Poetry, beauty, romance, love”
  • Individual voice — your verse
Quote bank
  • “Rip it out!”
  • “We're not laying pipe, we're talking about poetry.”
  • “This is a battle, a war. And the casualties could be your hearts and souls.”
  • “…poetry, beauty, romance, love, these are what we stay alive for.”
  • “What will your verse be?”
Why Keating is effective — and funny
  • Dramatic action: tearing the textbook
  • Deadpan reading, then one-word verdict
  • Mock-heroic war metaphor
  • Huddle and whisper — intimacy
  • Whitman's “O Me! O Life!” — authority
  • Closing rhetorical question
Asked in all three papers: individual desire vs social expectation (10m, 2024) · Keating as an effective speaker (6m, 2025) · elements of humour (6m, Amal 2025).
Module III · 8 hours · 15 marks

Reading

The Second ComingThe Frog PrinceOptimism (Part I)
★ Golden definition
“Reading is the ability to draw meaning from the printed page and interpret this information appropriately.”
— Grabe & Stoller (2002). Read in three layers: literal (what it says) → inferential (what it implies) → critical (what you think of it).

3.1The Second Coming — W. B. Yeats

Poem · two stanzas (8 + 14 lines)Written 1919 · published 1920Irish poet · Nobel Prize 1923

After the First World War, civilisation is spinning out of control; the speaker expects a “Second Coming” — not of Christ, but of a pitiless “rough beast” that will begin a new, violent age.

Stanza 1 — Chaos (what is)
  • “Turning and turning in the widening gyre / The falcon cannot hear the falconer;”
  • “Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;”
  • “The best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity.”
Stanza 2 — Revelation (what is coming)
  • “a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi”
  • “A shape with lion body and the head of a man, / A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun”
  • “what rough beast, its hour come round at last, / Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?”
SymbolMeaning
Widening gyreA spiral moving outward and losing control; Yeats's theory (A Vision) that history moves in 2,000-year cycles
Falcon / falconerHumanity / civilisation that can no longer hear its centre — God, Christ, reason, tradition
“Blood-dimmed tide”War, revolution and violence (WWI, Russian Revolution, Irish unrest)
“Ceremony of innocence”Rituals, decency and order that are now “drowned”
Spiritus MundiThe spirit of the world — a collective memory of images
Rough beast / BethlehemA sphinx-like anti-Christ born where Christ was born: the inversion of the Christian era
Asked: falcon and falconer (6m, 2025). Likely: “Things fall apart”; vision of chaos; the rough beast; title significance.

3.2The Frog Prince — James Finn Garner

Satirical retellingPolitically Correct Bedtime Stories, 1994American humorist

Garner retells the fairy tale in exaggerated politically correct jargon: the frog is a greedy developer, and the princess refuses both the castle and his land schemes.

Traditional tale (Grimm)Garner's “politically correct” version
The frogAn innocent prince under a witch's spellA property developer who swindled landlords — turned into a frog as punishment
The princessRewards the frog; marries himAn eco-feminist who sees through him
The castleHappy home, royal rewardSeat of “male power structures” — marriage, property, patriarchy
The ending“Happily ever after”Restored, he wants to develop more land; she ends his schemes by killing him
LanguageSimple fairy-tale styleInflated PC jargon — the source of the humour
What Garner satirises
  • Over-cautious political correctness and euphemism (link: Carlin)
  • Patriarchy in fairy tales — passive princesses
  • Greed and destruction of nature
  • Ideology taken to absurd extremes
Key terms
  • Eco-feminism: links the domination of women with the exploitation of nature
  • Parody: imitating a form to mock it
  • Role reversal: hero becomes villain
Before the examThe full text isn't available online. Copy the exact sentence containing “male power structures in the castle” from your textbook and quote it — Amal College asked about this phrase.
Asked: the princess as “eco-feminist warrior” (6m, Univ 2025) · “male power structures in the castle” (6m, Amal 2025).

3.3Optimism: An Essay, Part I “Optimism Within” — Helen Keller

Essay1903, written as a student at RadcliffeDeaf-blind from 19 months · taught by Anne Sullivan

If a woman who cannot see or hear is happy, her optimism is worth hearing: it is not ignorance of evil but a reasoned faith that good outweighs evil and must be worked for.

False optimism
  • “dangerous optimism of ignorance and indifference”
  • Comfortable people who saw “naught but good” while slaves were sold
  • “Hurrah, we're all right!”
  • “a house builded on sand”
Keller's true optimism
  • Knows evil and sorrow
  • “the struggle which evil necessitates is one of the greatest blessings”
  • “a glad belief in the preponderance of good”
  • “grounded in two worlds, myself and what is about me”
  • Work: “the desire and will to work is optimism itself”
Quote bank
  • “Darkness cannot shut me in again. I have had a glimpse of the shore, and can now live by the hope of reaching it.”
  • “the struggle which evil necessitates is one of the greatest blessings. It makes us strong, patient, helpful men and women.”
  • “I do live in a beautiful dream; but that dream is the actual, the present,—not cold, but warm;”
  • “Optimism that does not count the cost is like a house builded on sand.”
Asked: struggles with evil made her strong and patient (6m, 2024) · “Darkness cannot shut me in again” — her optimism (10m, Amal 2025).
Module IV · 8 hours · 15 marks

Writing

Ambedkar — Section 4The World-Renowned NoseReview of Cinema Paradiso
★ Golden definition
“Writing is the mental work of inventing ideas, thinking about how to express them, and organizing them into statements and paragraphs that will be clear to a reader.”
— David Nunan (2003). This module models three kinds of writing: argument (Ambedkar), satire (Basheer) and review (Cinema Paradiso).

4.1Annihilation of Caste, Section 4 — Dr B. R. Ambedkar

Argumentative speechPrepared 1936 for the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal, LahoreConference cancelled · Ambedkar self-published it

Defenders call caste a harmless “division of labour”; Ambedkar shows it is an unnatural, rigid and inefficient division of labourers that harms the economy.

BasisDivision of labour (civilised society)Caste system
DividesTasksPeople — into water-tight compartments
StructureHorizontalGraded hierarchy
Based onNatural aptitude, trainingBirth — social status of parents
MobilityFree to change jobs as industry changesForbidden — causes unemployment
ChoiceIndividual preference“dogma of predestination”
Quote bank
  • “Caste System is not merely division of labour. It is also a division of labourers.”
  • “What efficiency can there be in a system under which neither men's hearts nor their minds are in their work?”
  • “…the subordination of man's natural powers and inclinations to the exigencies of social rules.”
Never asked yet — the most likely new question. Practise: “Why is caste not merely a division of labour?” and a 10-mark essay on caste as an economic institution.

4.2The World-Renowned Nose — Vaikom Muhammad Basheer

Satirical short storyMalayalam Vishwavikhyathamaya Mookku, 1954“Beypore Sultan”, 1908–1994 · freedom fighter

An illiterate cook becomes a national celebrity, a political weapon and a scandal — all because his nose grows long; Basheer mocks the mob, the media and politicians who make heroes of nothing.

Social criticism
  • Mass psychology — “the mob is a stupid lot”, worshipping then trampling
  • Celebrity culture — fame without merit
  • Media sensationalism — reporters print his views on jets
  • Politics — parties chase publicity, not principles
  • Awards and universities honour the undeserving
  • Appropriation — everyone grabs a share of fame
Sources of humour
  • Absurd premise (fantasy)
  • Mock-historical tone — “the twenty-fourth year in the lives of all great men”
  • Hyperbole — 48 countries, tanks, riots
  • Parody of slogans — “Inquilab Zindabad!”
  • Irony — “Mookashri” echoes Padma Shri
  • Pathos beneath — a simple man used by all
Asked: social criticism and humour (10m, Amal 2025). Likely: mass psychology; political satire; Mookken's character.

4.3For the Love of Cinema — a review of Cinema Paradiso (1988)

Film reviewDir. Giuseppe Tornatore · ItalyMusic: Ennio MorriconeOscar, Best Foreign Language Film · Cannes Grand Prix

A famous director returns to his Sicilian village for the funeral of the projectionist who was his father-figure — a nostalgic love letter to cinema, childhood and lost love.

Anatomy of a review
  1. Title that hints at your verdict
  2. Details: title, year, director, language, cast
  3. Hook + short plot — no spoilers
  4. Analysis: acting, direction, screenplay, cinematography, music
  5. Themes and what the film means
  6. Strengths / weaknesses
  7. Verdict + rating and who should watch
Themes
  • Love of cinema as shared community experience
  • Childhood, memory and nostalgia
  • Mentorship: Alfredo and Totò
  • Censorship and freedom
  • The cost of ambition — leaving home
  • Lost love and change over time
Before the examThe prescribed review's web page is no longer online. Read it from your textbook and note two phrases the reviewer uses — quoting them shows you studied the text, not just the film.
Asked: write a short review of the film (6m, Amal 2025). Likely: review any film you have watched (creative); Alfredo–Totò relationship.

Identify the theme of family and relationships in Death of a Salesman.›10 marks★ Text asked 2×≈ 271 words

Asked in: Univ Nov 2025 Q17

Introduction. Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman (1949) is as much a family tragedy as a social one. The Loman family — Willy, Linda, Biff and Happy — is where the failure of the American Dream is felt most painfully.

Willy and Linda. In the opening scene Linda is endlessly patient. When Willy returns “tired to the death”, she suggests his glasses, urges him to ask Howard for a New York job, calls him “dear”, and absorbs his outbursts: “Why am I always being contradicted?” Miller notes that “she more than loves him, she admires him.” Her devotion sustains him but also shields him from the truth.

Willy and Biff. The central relationship is between father and elder son. Willy has staked his dreams on Biff, now 34 and drifting: “Not finding yourself at the age of thirty-four is a disgrace!” He calls him “a lazy bum” and then insists “he's not lazy” — love and disappointment in one breath. In the full play, Biff's discovery of Willy's affair destroys his faith in his father, and Willy's suicide for the insurance money is a final, misguided act of love.

Willy and Happy. The younger son, neglected, imitates his father's hollow talk of success.

Home and memory. “Work a lifetime to pay off a house. You finally own it, and there's nobody to live in it.” Willy idealises the past — the elm trees, Biff polishing the 1928 Chevvy — when the family seemed whole.

Conclusion. The Lomans love one another deeply, but their love is distorted by illusion and the pressure to succeed. Biff's verdict in the Requiem sums it up: Willy “had the wrong dreams.”

Examine Ambedkar's critique of the caste system as an economic institution (Section 4).›10 marksLikely · not yet asked≈ 272 words

Introduction. In 1936 Dr B. R. Ambedkar prepared a speech for the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal of Lahore; the conference was cancelled because the organisers objected to its content, and he published it himself as Annihilation of Caste. Section 4 answers a popular defence: that caste is merely “another name for division of labour”, which every civilised society needs.

Division of labourers, not labour. Ambedkar replies that “Caste System is not merely division of labour. It is also a division of labourers.” Civilised societies divide tasks, not people, and never into “water-tight compartments”. Caste is also a hierarchy in which labourers are graded one above another — found in no other country.

Not based on aptitude. Efficiency requires that people choose careers according to their trained capacities. Caste assigns work in advance according to the social status of the parents, violating this principle.

A cause of unemployment. Industry constantly changes, so workers must be free to change occupations. Caste forbids taking up work not inherited by birth; a Hindu may starve rather than take a new occupation. Thus caste becomes “a direct cause” of much unemployment.

Inefficiency and indignity. Caste ignores individual preference and rests on “the dogma of predestination”. Many occupations are stigmatised as degraded, so those who do them feel aversion and seek to escape. “What efficiency can there be in a system under which neither men's hearts nor their minds are in their work?”

Conclusion. Ambedkar concludes that caste is “a harmful institution” because it subordinates “man's natural powers and inclinations to the exigencies of social rules.” His economic argument remains relevant to debates on caste, employment and dignity of labour today.
